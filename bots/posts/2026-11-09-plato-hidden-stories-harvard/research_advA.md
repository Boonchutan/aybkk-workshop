## 2. Named asanas and why most are out

Already used (skip): Vasisthasana, Vishvamitrasana, Astavakrasana (Ep. 3), Hanumanasana (Ep. 5).
Out for safety or repeats:
- Bhairavasana: Bhairava's origin story is cutting off Brahma's head (AYI retells it from the Kurma Purana). Killing scene. Out.
- Ganda Bherundasana: Mysore royal emblem. Out (royalty).
- Skandasana: Skanda leads the gods' army (war). AYI's legend (Skanda destroys Brahma's world) has no source. The Ganesha-and-Skanda mango race is later Tamil folk lore, and Skanda is the one who loses. Out.
- Chakorasana: the chakora bird that drinks moonlight is a poetry habit, not a story. No old tale found. Out.
- Urdhva Kukkutasana (rooster): hen story already used for Kukkutasana. Out.
- Eka Pada Bakasana (crane): crane already used for Bakasana. The Panchatantra crane story is about eating fish. Out.
- Purna Matsyendrasana: Matsyendra already used. Out.
- Raja Kapotasana / Eka Pada Raja Kapotasana (pigeon): doves already used. King Shibi cuts his own flesh, and the Shanti Parva pigeon jumps into fire. Out.
- Viparita Salabhasana (locust or moth): the old image is the moth flying into the fire to die (Gita 11.29). Out.
- Supta / Utthita Trivikramasana: the famous Vamana story is about King Bali's kingdom and pushing him down (throne). Kept only as a reserve (see the end).
- Natarajasana: Patanjali and Vyaghrapada wait to see Shiva dance at Chidambaram. That is a Tamil temple legend (Koyil Puranam). I did not open an old text, so it is only a reserve.
- Kaundinya founding the kingdom of Funan (AYI, Kaundinyasana A): a royal founding story, set in Cambodia. Out.

Left, with a real old story: Durvasasana, Viranchyasana, Kaundinyasana, Kashyapasana, Galavasana.

## 3. The candidates (ranked)

### C1. Durvasasana: Mudgala and the guest who ate everything six times (best)
Name: durvasas = "badly clad", the name of a sage "known for his irascibility" (Monier-Williams p. 487).
The asana: you stand on one leg with the other foot behind your head. You rise up slowly from sitting, hands at the heart (AYI, Durvasana page).
Story (retold): Mudgala is a poor sage. He lives by picking up the grains left in the field after the harvest, "like a pigeon". For half a month he
collects one measure (a drona) of rice. On the moon days he feeds his guests first, then his wife and son eat what is left.
The sage Durvasa hears about him and comes looking wild, shouting rude words. He eats ALL the food.
Then he rubs the leftovers on his body and walks away. On the next moon day he comes back and does it again.
Six times in a row. Each time Mudgala does not eat. He just goes back to the field and picks up grains again.
No anger. Then Durvasa is pleased: it was a test. A sky chariot comes to take Mudgala to heaven. Mudgala asks
"what is bad about heaven?" The messenger tells him: you fall when your good deeds run out. Mudgala says no thank you,
and he stays with his practice (meditation) until he reaches the higher state.
Source: Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Ganguli sections CCLVIII to CCLIX (Gutenberg #15474, vol. 1). Critical edition 3.246 to 3.247 (GRETIL Sanskrit).
Exact words checked (Ganguli):
- "And he used to lead the Sila and Unchha modes of life. And although living like a pigeon, yet that one of mighty austerities entertained his guests"
- "having space alone for his covering, his accoutrements worn like that of maniac, and his head bare of hair, came there, uttering, O Pandava various insulting words"
- "Then having eaten up all that food, he besmeared his body with the unclean orts and went away as he had come."
- "Thereupon, without partaking any food himself, the sage Mudgala again became engaged in collecting corn"
- "Hunger could not disturb his equanimity. Nor could anger, nor guile, nor a sense of degradation, nor agitation, enter into the heart"
- "In this way, Durvasa having made up his mind, during successive seasons presented himself for six several times"
- "I do not desire heaven." ... "he became always engaged in meditation"
- Sanskrit 3.246.21: "upatasthe yathākālaṃ ṣaṭkṛtvaḥ kṛtaniścayaḥ" (he came at the right time, six times, his mind made up). 3.246.18: "dvitīye saṃprāpte parvakāle" (when the second moon day came).
Surprise: almost nobody knows Mudgala. The famous Durvasa stories are about his curses. Here the "angry" sage is the tester, and the hero is the one who starts again with nothing, six times. Then he says no to heaven.
Practice lesson: Durvasasana goes wrong almost every time. You wobble, you come down. The lesson is not "never fall". It is the same calm each time you start again. Rise slowly (AYI says rise slowly on the inhale; hands on the thigh help).
Habit Gap: progress gets "eaten" (a sick week, a trip, a bad month). People quit in anger at themselves. Mudgala goes back to the field the same day, without anger.
Hook ideas: #107 adapted ("'Start over' is not your problem. Being angry is." flips "a bad week ruins my practice"). Alt #101 (strict often means someone is paying attention: the rude guest was the test).
Lane B (comment aybkk).
Safety: no killing, no war, no throne. Durvasa is "naked" in the text: say "came looking wild", never naked. Leave out Kurukshetra (it is the battlefield). Do not mock Durvasa: the text says he came on purpose to test, and he praises Mudgala, so tell him as the tester.
Do not say "12 weeks". Six moon days is about three months, but that is my math, not the text.

### C2. Viranchyasana: the creator heard two sounds, "ta... pa"
Name: viriñca = "name of Brahmā" (Monier-Williams p. 983). AYI: "Virañcya ist ein Beiname von Brahmā".
The asana: A = one leg in half lotus, the other foot behind the head, sit tall, hands at the heart. B = sitting on one heel, folding forward (AYI).
Story (retold): Brahma, the creator, wakes up on top of a lotus. He does not know who he is or what to do.
He climbs down inside the lotus stem into the dark water to find where it comes from. He searches a very long time and finds nothing.
So he comes back up to his own seat. He slows his breath. He sits. And after a long time he sees in his heart what he could not find by searching.
The other telling: while Brahma is thinking, he hears a voice in the water say two sounds, twice. Ta. Pa. "Tapa" means heat, the hard practice.
He looks everywhere. Nobody is there. So he sits down on his lotus and practises for a thousand years of the gods. Then Vishnu shows himself.
Sources: Bhagavata Purana 3.8.18 to 3.8.22 and 2.9.5 to 2.9.9 (vedabase.io, Prabhupada translation; Sanskrit checked on the same pages).
Exact words checked:
- 3.8.19: "entered the water through the channel of the stem of the lotus. But in spite of entering the stem ... he could not trace out the root."
- 3.8.21: "he retired from such searching and came back again to the top of the lotus". Sanskrit: "sva-dhiṣṇyam āsādya punaḥ ... śanair jita-śvāsa ... nyaṣīdad" (reaching his own seat again ... slowly controlling the breath ... he sat down). Monier-Williams: śanais = "quietly, softly, gently, gradually" (the vedabase gloss "without delay" is weaker).
- 3.8.22: "he could see in his heart the Supreme within himself, whom he could not see before with the greatest endeavor." Sanskrit "apaśyatāpaśyata yan na pūrvam".
- 2.9.6: "Brahmājī heard twice from nearby two syllables joined together ... the sixteenth and the twenty-first of the sparśa alphabets". Count it yourself in the alphabet: ka to ma, the 16th is ta and the 21st is pa. So the word is "tapa".
- 2.9.7: "he tried to find the speaker, searching on all sides. But when he was unable to find anyone ... he thought it wise to sit down on his lotus seat firmly and give his attention to the execution of penance".
- 2.9.8: "Lord Brahmā underwent penances for one thousand years by the calculations of the demigods."
- tapas = "warmth, heat ... religious austerity" (Monier-Williams p. 437).
Surprise: the god who made the world did not know what to do. His first lesson was two sounds, "practise", and it came from nobody he could see. And searching outside did not work; sitting back down on his own seat did.
Practice lesson: Viranchyasana A is a seat. Leg behind the head, sit tall, slow breath. The asana is about coming back to your seat and calming the breath, not chasing the shape.
Habit Gap: people go "down the stem" looking for a new method, a new app, a new teacher. The answer was: come back to your own seat and practise. Same practice, same place.
Hook ideas: #13 adapted ("The complete guide to Ashtanga is two syllables long."). Alt #60 (wasting time deciding which workout to do).
Lane B.
Safety: fine. Respectful to Brahma: the Bhagavata itself says he could not find the root, so tell it the text's way, never as a joke. Say "a thousand years of the gods", not a number of human years. Do not say "100 years" for the stem search. 3.8.20 says only that a very long time passed.

### C3. Kaundinyasana: Kondañña, the one finger, the five who left, the one who understood first
Name: Kauṇḍinya is a family (clan) name of sages (Monier-Williams p. 315, with a cross-reference to "Ājñāta-Kauṇḍinya", the Buddha's first student). Pali form: Koṇḍañña.
AYI's Kaundinyasana B page links the asana to the Buddhist Kauṇḍinya and the prophecy at the birth of Siddhartha. Honest line for the script: "Kaundinya is an old family name of sages. One man with this name..." No old book says the asana is named after the Buddha's student.
The asana: A = arm balance, both legs to one side. B = arm balance, one leg forward over the arm, one leg back and up (AYI).
Story (retold): when baby Siddhartha is born, eight wise men look at him. Seven hold up two fingers: maybe a great king, maybe a Buddha.
The youngest, Kondañña, holds up ONE finger: he will be a Buddha. Years later Kondañña and four friends follow the young seeker.
They serve him for six years, sweeping his place. Then he starts to eat rice again, to find the middle way. The five think he has given up.
They leave him and walk far away to the deer park near Benares. After he wakes up (becomes the Buddha), he thinks: who should I teach first?
He remembers the five, "they were of great service to me", and he walks all the way to find them. They agree not to stand up for him.
But when he comes near, they cannot keep it. One takes his bowl, one makes his seat, one brings water for his feet. He teaches them.
Kondañña understands first. The Buddha says "Koṇḍañña has understood!" That becomes his name: Añña Koṇḍañña, "Kondañña who understood".
Sources: (a) Jataka Nidanakatha, Rhys Davids, Buddhist Birth Stories vol. 1 (1880), Gutenberg #51880, pp. 72-73 and the Great Struggle part.
(b) Vinaya, Mahavagga (Khandhaka 1), section 6, Brahmali translation, SuttaCentral pli-tv-kd1:6.5 to 6.31 (also told in SN 56.11).
Exact words checked:
- Nidanakatha: "Seven of them holding up two fingers prophesied in the alternative" ... "the youngest of all of them, a young Brāhman whose family name was Kondanya ... raised up one finger only, and prophesied without ambiguity"
- Nidanakatha: "for six years they stayed by him and served him ... such as sweeping out the hermitage"
- Nidanakatha: "He is altogether lost in the Struggle. ... like a man, who wants to bathe his head, thinking of using a dew-drop" ... "leaving the Great Being, they took each his robes and begging bowl, and went eighteen leagues away, and entered Isipatana"
- Vinaya kd1:6.5.5: "The group of five monks who supported me while I was striving were of great service to me."
- Vinaya kd1:6.10.4-6.11.1: "We shouldn't bow down to him, stand up for him, or receive his bowl and robe" ... "But as the Buddha approached, the group of five monks was unable to keep the agreement."
- Vinaya kd1:6.31.5-6: "Koṇḍañña has understood! Indeed, Koṇḍañña has understood!" ... "That's how Koṇḍañña got the name 'Aññāsikoṇḍañña'" (Pali "aññāsi vata bho koṇḍañño").
Surprise: the first man to believe was also one of the five who walked away. He thought his teacher had quit. And the one who came back was the first to understand. Most people outside Thailand never heard this. (In Thailand, Asanha Bucha day marks this first teaching, so Thai viewers may know the end. The one finger and the walking away are less known.)
Practice lesson: in Kaundinyasana, too much force and you tip over, too little and you fall back. You find the middle. The five left because they believed only the hardest way was real.
Habit Gap: the strongest of all the candidates. They quit. The teacher remembered them and came to find them. The first one back understood first. Coming back is not failing.
Hook ideas: #121 Triple Hook (the story shows the opposite of "nobody notices"). Alt #83 (how do you start over after starting and quitting?).
Lane B.
Safety: careful, the Buddha is in it. Respectful only. NEVER compare Boonchu or AYBKK to the Buddha. Say "even the Buddha remembered the five", and the AYBKK line stays separate ("at AYBKK a teacher notices when you miss class").
No AI picture of the Buddha in the Kling images (Thai sensitivity about Buddha images in ads): show the deer park, five bowls, a footprint path, or the monks from behind.
Leave out "Universal Monarch" (royalty) and the king. Do not say a distance in km. "Eighteen leagues" is in the book, but that number means nothing to viewers.

### C4. Kashyapasana: the teacher who saw a sad face and gave twelve days of practice
Name: Kaśyapa, an ancient sage, husband of Aditi, father of the Adityas (Monier-Williams p. 265). AYI: "Pose Named after Kaśyapa".
The asana: one foot behind the head, then you lie down flat on your back. AYI: "Pattabhi Jois wollte, dass das gestreckte Bein (links) auf dem Boden bleibt, auch wenn sich dabei das Knie etwas an beugt. Mit der Übungspraxis kannst Du dieses mehr und mehr strecken" (Pattabhi Jois wanted the straight leg to stay on the floor even if the knee bends a little. With practice you can straighten it more and more). That is AYI's secondhand report, so flag it if used.
Story (retold): the sage Kashyapa comes home after a long meditation. His home has no joy. He looks at his wife Aditi's face and says: I can see you are not well.
She tells him her children have lost their home. She asks him to fix it. He smiles. He does not give her a miracle. He gives her a practice:
twelve days, every day, the same simple vow and worship. She does it for twelve days "without laziness". On the end of it, Vishnu appears before her.
Source: Bhagavata Purana 8.16.1 to 8.16.25, 8.16.58 to 62, 8.17.1 to 8.17.4 (vedabase.io).
Exact words checked:
- 8.16.2: "returned home to see the āśrama of Aditi neither jubilant nor festive." Sanskrit "nirutsavaṁ nirānandaṁ".
- 8.16.10: "Seeing your withered face, I can perceive that your mind is not tranquil." Sanskrit "lakṣaye 'svastham ātmānaṁ bhavatyā lakṣaṇair aham" (I notice by the signs that you are not well).
- 8.16.18: "When Kaśyapa Muni was thus requested by Aditi, he slightly smiled."
- 8.16.25: "for twelve days ending with Dvādaśī, one should observe the vow of subsisting only on milk".
- 8.17.1: "she strictly followed his instructions without laziness" Sanskrit "dvādaśāham atandritā" (for twelve days, unwearied).
- 8.17.4: "the original Supreme Personality of Godhead ... then appeared before Aditi."
Surprise: the great sage is asked for a miracle and hands out homework. And the first thing he does is notice her face.
Practice lesson: Kashyapasana does not open in one day. Keep the straight leg down even with a bent knee, and it straightens over many mornings.
Habit Gap: "a teacher who notices" is in the text word for word (lakṣaye, "I notice"). The fix is a small daily practice, not a wish.
Hook ideas: #117 adapted (the part of teaching nobody sees: noticing a face). Alt #6.
Lane B.
Risks: the background is war. In 8.15 the asuras (demons) took heaven, and Aditi's sons lost their home. Say only "her children lost their home", never the fight. The story goes on to Vamana and King Bali: stop at "Vishnu appeared".
Milk-only is a fasting vow: never present it as diet advice. Say "a simple vow". It is husband and wife, not teacher and student, so say "Kashyapa, a wise teacher, came home to his wife". Do not link the 12 days to the AYBKK 12-week check as if the text meant it.

### C5. Galavasana: the student who stayed, and the teacher who said "Go"
Name: Gālava, "an old sage ... pupil (MBh.) of Viśvāmitra" (Monier-Williams p. 354). AYI: "Pose dedicated to Gālava".
The asana: an arm balance, one shin on the upper arms, the other leg long, up and back (AYI).
Story (retold): a god comes, dressed as a sage, to test Vishvamitra, and asks for food. Vishvamitra cooks rice so carefully that he is too late: the guest has already eaten.
The guest says "wait here" and leaves. So Vishvamitra stands still holding the hot pot on his head, for a hundred years. His student Galava looks after him all that time.
The guest comes back. The food is still warm. He eats and is pleased. Vishvamitra tells Galava: you are free, go where you like. Galava asks: what gift can I give you?
The teacher says "Go, go." Galava keeps asking. The teacher gets a little angry: "Bring me eight hundred white horses, each with one black ear."
Stop the story there.
Source: Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva, Ganguli section CVI (Gutenberg #15475, vol. 2).
Exact words checked:
- "in consequence of the care he took in preparing that excellent food, he could not properly wait upon his guest"
- "'I have already dined; wait here,'--were the words that the holy one said."
- "bearing that food on his head and holding it with his arms, that ascetic of rigid vow stood in his hermitage, still as a post, subsisting on air"
- "an ascetic of the name of Galava, from motives of respect and reverence and from affection and desire of doing what was agreeable, began to wait upon him. And after a hundred years had passed away"
- "Dharma accepted that food which was still warm and fresh"
- "With my leave, O Galava, go whithersoever thou mayest wish." ... "the Rishi, therefore, sought to dismiss him by repeatedly saying, 'Go, Go.'"
- "Give me eight hundred steeds, every one of which should be as white as the rays of the moon, and every one of which should have one ear black."
Surprise: a teacher who stands still for a hundred years with warm food. A student nobody talks about, who served the whole time. And then too much eagerness gets him into trouble.
Practice lesson: when the teacher says "enough, go home", go home. Do not insist on the next asana. Galavasana is held still, not pushed.
Habit Gap: it fits weakly. It is about pushing too hard after the habit is built, not about quitting. Better as Lane A (the "stuck, my teacher won't give me the next asana" crowd).
Hook ideas: #106 ("I don't want to give you new asanas just to keep you interested."). Alt #14.
Lane A (comment STUCK on the end card, never spoken).
Risks (HIGH): what happens next is the Madhavi story: a king's daughter is handed to three kings to bear sons in exchange for horses. Not bedtime, and AYI itself adds a disclaimer about it. Viewers who know the story may ask why we chose it, so never tell what happens next.
The text tells it as a lesson against stubbornness, told to Duryodhana before the war. Leave out the frame. Also leave out the line about caste ("divested of Kshatriyahood").
Vishvamitra is already in Ep. 3 era stories (stories-used has "Vishvamitra loses his tapas"), so this would be the second Vishvamitra episode.

## 4. Reserves (not checked enough to use)
- Supta Trivikramasana, oldest dwarf story, Shatapatha Brahmana 1.2.5 (Eggeling, SBE 12; read on the neonvagabond.xyz mirror of sacred-texts, since sacred-texts itself is blocked).
  "As much as this Vishnu lies upon, and no more, we give you!" ... "Now Vishnu was a dwarf." ... the gods "were not offended" ...
  "they went on worshipping and toiling with it ... By it they obtained this entire earth" ... "Thereupon this Vishnu became tired".
  No King Bali, and the dwarf is LYING down (supta). Lesson: small thing plus daily work. Risks: gods and asuras dividing land, "rivals" (land and territory talk). It is a ritual text, and it has no three strides, so the link to Trivikrama is only half there.
- Natarajasana: Vyaghrapada ("tiger foot") picks flowers before dawn every day and waits with Patanjali to see Shiva dance. Tamil temple legend (Koyil Puranam / Chidambara Mahatmya). Not opened, so it is modern lore until checked. Upamanyu (Ep. 6) is called a "descendant of Vyaghrapada" in Anushasana Parva 14, so it could be a sequel, but do not say they are the same family without a source.
- Raja Kapotasana: Mudgala lives "like a pigeon" (kapota way of life). This is a possible second link for C1, but doves were already used for Kapotasana.
