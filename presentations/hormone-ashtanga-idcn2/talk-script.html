Ashtanga Intensity & the Chemistry of Transformation

Hormones, Habit & the Nervous System — for Indepth China #2
~20 minutes · 18 slides · Companion to index.html · Speak slower than feels natural — precision is authority.

How to hold the room — presence & boundary

You know these ten well. Some of them drive you around, share meals, feel like friends. Tonight, shift the register slightly: less friend, more teacher. Warmth in the eyes, steadiness in the body — and let the silences sit. Don't fill the pause. Don't fish for the nod.

When you drop a piece of science they've never met, don't soften it into a question — state it, then wait. The goal isn't to impress them; it's to let them feel the floor beneath the practice they thought they already knew. That felt gap — "there is so much here I don't have" — is what re-sets the distance, without a single cold word.

Speak slower than feels natural. You are not offering opinions tonight; you are showing them the machinery. Leave the last slide in silence for one full breath before you close.

Pace: ~1 min per slide, 1.5–2 on the big ones. Total ≈ 20 minutes, leaving room for their questions after.
01

Title — the frame

0:00
Say
"For years, you have felt what this practice does to you. Tonight I want to show you why — underneath the postures, in the chemistry and in the nervous system. This is not a class about asana. It's about the machinery that the asana has been quietly training, all this time." 今晚，我们走到体式之下。
02

The endocrine event

0:40
Say
"Everything you feel on the mat begins as an endocrine event. Your body detects a stimulus — 刺激 — and answers by releasing a hormone, to pull itself back toward balance. Stress, effort, fasting, emotion — each one is a signal. The practice is simply a way of sending those signals, on purpose, every single morning."
Depth-drop
The word 'balance' is almost a lie. The body is never still — it's constantly overcorrecting. What practice trains is not stillness; it's the quality of the correction.
03

Practice reacts chemically

1:50
Say
"So when you practice, the body doesn't just move — it answers, chemically. Four answers. Effort asks for testosterone and growth hormone. Stress and challenge call cortisol. Focus and breath raise dopamine and serotonin. And fatigue, surrender — endorphins."
Ask
"A question. You feel powerful after backbends — clear, strong, almost taller. Which of these four is that? … And why is it gone by the evening?"
Let them try. Don't rescue them. The answer comes on slide 6 — the rise is real but small and short-lived. Sit in the gap.
Note to self
These four links are memory-hooks, not literal wiring. If a student pushes, concede warmly: "Yes — it's a way to remember; the real pathways overlap." Conceding precisely raises your authority, it doesn't lower it.
04

The thesis

3:10
Say
"If you take one line from tonight, take this. Ritual, plus intensity, plus repetition — done long enough — becomes neurochemical change. Not motivation. Not willpower. Chemistry, and wiring. That is why this practice changes people who never intended to change."
05

Hormonal mechanics — the map

3:50
Say
"Four messengers. Testosterone — power, drive, growth. Cortisol — metabolism and stress regulation. Dopamine — motivation and learning. Serotonin — satisfaction and balance. We'll take them one at a time. Watch how each one has a different job — and a different feel in your body."
06

Testosterone & growth

4:30
Say
"Testosterone first. Physical intensity — jump-backs, long holds, the fatigue of a led class — the body reads that as a signal to build. Isometric intensity, muscle recruitment, an anabolic signal. 合成代谢信号。"
Depth-drop
Be precise here, because students will over-believe it. The testosterone rise from a practice is small, and brief — minutes to about an hour. It is not what builds the muscle; the building is mostly local, in the tissue itself. The hormone is a whisper, not the whole story. A teacher who says "yoga floods you with testosterone" is selling, not teaching. (This is the answer to the backbend question.)
07

Cortisol & controlled stress

5:50
Say
"Cortisol. All of wellness treats it as the enemy — it isn't. In the right dose it's the sharpening hormone. Mysore — 迈索尔 — is a controlled adrenal challenge. Fear and challenge raise cortisol and testosterone together. Cortisol mobilizes energy, sharpens the response, and lowers inflammation."
Depth-drop
The whole secret is one word: acute. Everything good I just said is true of a short, sharp rise. Chronic cortisol — from a life that never downshifts — reverses all of it: it eats muscle, suppresses immunity, raises blood sugar. So the practice is not "low cortisol." It's a clean spike, then a real drop. That drop is the skill.
08

Hormesis

7:10
Say
"That clean spike-then-drop has a name: hormesis. 适应性压力。 Small, repeated, controlled stress that makes the system stronger. This is why Mysore is self-paced. You pace yourself, you push within limits, and — the hard one — you learn to recover while still in effort. Marichyasana D. Kapotasana. You stay in the fire, and you find your breath there."
Ask
"So — where, in your own practice, do you actually recover while still working? Not resting between poses. Recovering, inside the effort."
Most won't be able to answer precisely — good. That skill is the next years of practice. Name that plainly.
09

Dopamine & presence

8:40
Say
"Dopamine. Most people think it's the reward at the end — landing the pose. It isn't. Dopamine rises during the process of getting there. Learning a new transition. A jump-through that improves by an inch. Tracking the breath with real precision. That's why practice can feel good on the way — and why chasing the finish quietly kills it."
Depth-drop
Cold showers raise dopamine too — and unlike most things, the rise is slow, and lasts for hours, not a quick spike-and-crash. That's the same shape as practice. The nervous system prefers the slow climb — the fast hits are the ones that cost you.
10

Serotonin & mood

10:00
Say
"Serotonin is the sustainability hormone. It doesn't spike — it rises with rhythm, repetition, steady progress. Same time, same room, same order, every day. And it needs raw material: tryptophan — 色氨酸 — an essential amino acid your body cannot make. You have to eat it: tofu, eggs, seeds. From that, the body builds serotonin."
Depth-drop
Say this carefully, or a sharp student will catch you. Most of the body's serotonin lives in the gut, and it doesn't cross into the brain. Eating tofu does not directly lift your mood. Tryptophan is raw material — necessary, but not a switch. The honest claim is always "the raw material," never "eat this and feel happy."
11

Neuroscience of habit — the insula

11:30
Say
"Now leave the hormones, and go to the wiring. The nervous system is the foundation — it builds the muscle, through the mind–muscle link. Practice forms cortical maps of movement. And every time you feel the muscles, the joints, the heartbeat, the fatigue — you are training the insula, 脑岛: body-awareness, pain tolerance, emotional regulation, self-observation."
Depth-drop
The insula is buried deep, folded under the surface of the brain — you cannot see it from outside. Interoception — feeling inward — is the one sense we never even name, and it's the one this practice trains hardest. Most people live an entire life with a quiet insula. You are the exceptions.
12

Cortical map vs muscle memory

13:00
Say
"Here is the distinction to keep. Cortical mapping is the conscious sculpting — you, deliberately, building the model: what to move, how to move it, how it should feel. Muscle memory is the subconscious result. You sculpt with attention; the body keeps the copy."
Note to self
This split is teaching, not strict anatomy — "muscle memory" actually lives in the brain (basal ganglia, cerebellum), not the muscle. If someone knows that, agree, and keep the metaphor: conscious effort now becomes unconscious skill later.
13

Do one part at a time

14:10
Say
"A practical secret — for the hard postures, and for discipline itself. The difficult thing? Do one part at a time. 一次，只做一部分。 Break it. And here is the mechanism: each part you complete gives a small dopamine kick, which pulls you toward the next. That is not a mood trick — that is how the brain builds a habit."
14

Tristana

15:10
Say
"This is our technology for that attention. Tristana — 三处专注 — gaze, breath, and bandha, held together. Drishti fixes the eyes. Ujjayi fixes the breath. Bandha holds the core. Three streams into one point. That convergence is what the lab calls cognitive-motor integration — mind and movement, fused. The old method already knew what science is only now naming."
15

Cortisol as fuel

16:20
Say
"Back to cortisol, practically — what it does with fuel. It mobilizes: raises blood sugar for ready energy, releases fat for steady fuel, and breaks protein down for backup. Notice the word — breaks down. Cortisol is a spender, not a saver."
Depth-drop
So where is recovery? Recovery is not high cortisol. Recovery comes when cortisol falls — when you finish, breathe, rest — and the anabolic, repair state takes over. The old rhythm — a strong practice, then a real savasana, food, sleep — is the endocrinology, done right long before anyone had the words.
16

Hormones aren't results

17:40
Say
"So — the reframe. Stop chasing the hormone like a prize. Hormones aren't results. 激素不是结果。 They are part of the process — the weather inside the work, not the trophy at the end. The student who practices to get the dopamine has already lost it."
17

Laboratory of the nervous system

18:30
Say
"Which leaves us here. Ashtanga is a laboratory of the nervous system. Every morning on the mat is an experiment you run on yourself — same variables, small changes, careful observation. You are the scientist and the subject. Six days a week, for years."
18

Close

19:20
Say — slower
"So. Practice isn't just movement. It's a nervous and endocrine event. It began that way on our first slide — and that is what it still is: far more than a body moving through shapes. It is you — quietly rewriting your own chemistry."
Pause. Let the silence sit for one full breath.
"…That is where we begin."

If a sharp student pushes back — honest answers

Conceding a limit precisely is the most authoritative thing you can do. Never bluff; use these.

"Doesn't yoga flood you with testosterone?"
No — the rise is small and lasts under an hour, and muscle is built mostly locally. The hormone is a signal, not the cause.
"So eating tryptophan makes me happy?"
Not directly. Most serotonin is in the gut and doesn't reach the brain. Tryptophan is necessary raw material — not a mood switch.
"Isn't muscle memory in the muscle?"
No — it lives in the brain (basal ganglia, cerebellum, motor cortex). "Muscle memory" is a useful name, not an address.
"Isn't cortisol bad for you?"
Acute cortisol sharpens; chronic cortisol harms. The practice's value is the clean spike and the drop after.
