## 1. Maria Montessori · big word MOVEMENT · educator · quote
Quote: "But mental development must be connected with movement and is dependent on it. This is the new idea that must enter educational theory and practice."
Credit: Maria Montessori · The Absorbent Mind, 1949 (first edition, Adyar), ch. XIII, p. 203
Plain meaning: Montessori told teachers that the growth of a child's mind is tied to movement and depends on it.
Who: Italian physician and educator (1870-1952) who founded the Montessori method of teaching young children.
Source: https://archive.org/details/absorbentmind031961mbp (primary (full text read and the printed page 203 checked on the scan))
Context: Montessori is telling teachers in training that schools wrongly treat movement as something only for bodily health. She says the brain, the senses and the muscles are one system and must work together. ALSO VERIFIED on p. 204: "Watching him, one sees that he develops his mind by using his movements." and "...the child, scientifically observed, shows that he develops his intelligence generally through movement."; in ch. XIV "Intelligence and the Hand": "The development of the hand therefore goes side by side with the development of the intelligence."
Cautions: Two English wordings are in print. The later standard edition, translated from the Italian by Claude A. Claremont, reads: "...mental development must be connected with movement and be dependent on it. It is vital that educational theory and practice should become informed by this idea." Name the 1949 edition on the slide. The slide cut starts mid-sentence (the sentence opens with "But"). Her "development of the brain" is a teacher's claim from the 1940s about small children, not a measurement. Keep to pp. 203-204: nearby pages talk about heaven and reincarnation and use a "Minister of Foreign Affairs" image. Biographies note her schools had Italian state backing in the 1920s before she left Italy in 1934 (general knowledge, not checked here). A different Montessori line was used before (the 44 repetitions).
Last check: None failed. Both parts of the long quote are exact against the page scan. "must" is in italics in the book. Always credit the 1949 Adyar edition: the later standard English edition (translated from her Italian rewrite) has different words. Plain meaning tightened to the words of this line ("connected with movement and is dependent on it"). Not in used.txt (a different Montessori line was used before).

## 2. Roger W. Sperry · big word ACTION · science · quote
Quote: "In both its phylogenetic and ontogenetic histories, mental activity develops out of, and in reference to, overt action."
Credit: Roger W. Sperry · "Neurology and the Mind-Brain Problem", American Scientist 40(2), April 1952, p. 299
Plain meaning: A brain scientist who later won a Nobel Prize wrote that thinking grows out of doing, both in the long history of animals and in every growing child.
Who: American brain scientist (1913-1994), Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1981 for split-brain research. He wrote this paper at the University of Chicago.
Source: https://people.uncw.edu/puente/sperry/sperrypapers/50s/46-1952.pdf (primary: scan of the author's reprint of the journal article (cover leaf: "Reprinted from the American Scientist Vol. 40, No. 2, April, 1952"). Pages 297-299 rendered and read as images by the researcher and again by the checker.)
Context: Sperry argues that to understand the mind, science should look at what the brain puts out, and that output is movement. "Phylogenetic and ontogenetic" means: in the history of species and in the growth of each child. Same paper, pp. 297-298, also read: "...the sole product of brain function is motor coordination. To repeat: the entire output of our thinking machine consists of nothing but patterns of motor coordination."
Cautions: A theory paper from 1952, not an experiment and not about exercise training. Two hard words (phylogenetic, ontogenetic) need a plain gloss. In the very next sentence Sperry adds that mind and movement depend on each other ("cyclic and reciprocal interdependence") and that he stresses the motor side on purpose. "Sole product" in the second line is his deliberate overstatement: present it as his view. In that second line the words from "the entire output" to "motor coordination" are in italics in the original.
Last check: Text exact. Locator made precise: it is the last sentence of the paragraph that begins "Perceptions and ideas are found" (second full paragraph on p. 299). Plain meaning corrected: the Nobel Prize came in 1981, after this 1952 paper, so "later won". If the short cut is used it starts mid-sentence, so print it with dots: "...mental activity develops out of, and in reference to, overt action." "Overt action" means visible movement and behaviour, not exercise: do not caption it as a line about exercise.

## 3. Aristotle · big word BEFORE · philosopher · quote
Quote: "Now it is clear that in education practice must be used before theory, and the body be trained before the mind"
Original: ἐπεὶ δὲ φανερὸν πρότερον τοῖς ἔθεσιν ἢ τῷ λόγῳ παιδευτέον εἶναι, καὶ περὶ τὸ σῶμα πρότερον ἢ τὴν διάνοιαν
Credit: Aristotle · Politics VIII.3 (1338b4-6), about 330 BC · tr. Benjamin Jowett, revised by W. D. Ross (Oxford, 1921)
Plain meaning: Aristotle said that in bringing up children, practice comes before theory and training the body comes before training the mind.
Who: Greek philosopher (384-322 BC), student of Plato and founder of the Lyceum school in Athens.
Source: https://archive.org/details/theworksofaristo10arisuoft ; https://archive.org/details/worksofaristotle0000wdro_f0a6 ; 1885 edition: https://archive.org/details/politicsofaristo01arisuoft ; web copy: http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.8.eight.html ; Greek: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/master/data/tlg0086/tlg035/tlg0086.tlg035.perseus-grc2.xml (primary: read by two people in three scans of the 1921 Oxford volume and in Jowett's 1885 first edition (archive.org scan text), and in the Greek (W. D. Ross, Oxford Classical Text, Perseus file; second copy el.wikisource))
Context: Aristotle names the four usual school subjects (reading and writing, gymnastic exercises, music, drawing) and fixes the order: habits before reasoning, the body before the mind. The next chapter (VIII.4) at once warns against hard training for children: exercises 'of a lighter kind, avoiding severe diet or painful toil, lest the growth of the body be impaired'.
Cautions: Two real wordings exist: name the edition. Jowett's own 1885 text reads 'habit must go before reason, and the body before the mind'; the 'practice ... trained' wording is Ross's 1921 revision (the one on most websites). The Internet Classics Archive copy has typing errors ('roper habit'): copy from the print text. A critic can cite the next chapter, 1339a7-10: 'the labour of the body impedes the mind, and the labour of the mind the body' (he means not both hard at the same time). Aristotle speaks of the order of schooling for boys, not of brain growth: do not add the word 'brain'.
Last check: Quote is exact in the 1921 revised text. Corrections: (1) The locator's '1885 first edition, vol. I, p. 248' has DIFFERENT words: "in education habit must go before reason, and the body before the mind". Never credit the printed wording to 'Jowett 1885'; credit 'Jowett, revised by Ross, 1921'. (2) The MIT web copy has a typo ('roper habit'); do not copy from it. (3) Greek says literally 'by habits before by reasoning'; 'practice before theory' is Ross's wording. (4) Plain line changed: the source says 'in education', not 'a school'. (5) Caption should stay honest: the next chapter warns against hard training for children. Final cut ends at the semicolon; the sentence goes on about the trainer and wrestling-master.

## 4. Jean-Jacques Rousseau · big word TOOLS · educator · quote
Quote: "To learn to think we must therefore exercise our limbs, our senses, and our bodily organs, which are the tools of the intellect"
Original: Pour apprendre à penser, il faut donc exercer nos membres, nos sens, nos organes, qui sont les instruments de notre intelligence
Credit: Jean-Jacques Rousseau · Emile, or On Education, 1762, Book II · tr. Barbara Foxley, 1911
Plain meaning: Rousseau said our arms, legs and senses are the tools we think with, so we must use them to learn to think.
Who: Geneva-born writer and philosopher (1712-1778); his book Emile changed how Europe thought about bringing up children.
Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5427/pg5427.txt | French: https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Émile,_ou_De_l'éducation/Édition_1852/Livre_II | 1911 scan: https://archive.org/details/emile0000rous (primary (full text of the translation and of the French read; the 1911 printed page checked by search inside the scan))
Context: Rousseau speaks as the tutor of the boy Emile, in the part about childhood up to about twelve. He argues that a child should first learn through the body and the senses, not from books. Two sentences earlier he writes "our first teachers in natural philosophy are our feet, hands, and eyes" (« nos premiers maîtres de philosophie sont nos pieds, nos mains, nos yeux »). ALSO VERIFIED in the same Book, a few pages earlier (Everyman p. 82): "Would you cultivate your pupil's intelligence, cultivate the strength it is meant to control. Give his body constant exercise, make it strong and healthy, in order to make him good and wise; let him work, let him do things, let him run and shout, let him be always on the go; make a man of him in strength, and he will soon be a man in reason." (« Voulez-vous donc cultiver l'intelligence de votre élève ; cultivez les forces qu'elle doit gouverner. Exercez continuellement son corps ; rendez-le robuste et sain, pour le rendre sage et raisonnable ; qu'il travaille, qu'il agisse, qu'il coure, qu'il crie, qu'il soit toujours en mouvement ; qu'il soit homme par la vigueur, et bientôt il le sera par la raison. ») and "It is a lamentable mistake to imagine that bodily activity hinders the working of the mind, as if these two kinds of activity ought not to advance hand in hand, and as if the one were not intended to act as guide to the other."
Cautions: The wording passed around online is slightly off ("...the body which supplies us with these tools..."); Foxley prints "with them". He writes about a child, not adults. Right after the "cultivate" passage he compares "peasants and savages" (dated words): stop the cut before that. Critics note Rousseau left his own children at a foundling home, and Book V of Emile on girls is dated.
Last check: None failed. English and French exact. The cut stops at a semicolon in the source. Page 90 confirmed in the Everyman scan (and p. 82 for "Would you cultivate your pupil's intelligence..."). Not in used.txt.

## 5. Gerd Kempermann and colleagues (Kempermann, Fabel, Ehninger, Babu, Leal-Galicia, Garthe, Wolf) · big word BASIS · science · quote
Quote: "In animals, most if not all aspects of cognition are inseparable from locomotion and physical activity."
Credit: Gerd Kempermann and colleagues · "Why and how physical activity promotes experience-induced brain plasticity", Frontiers in Neuroscience 4:189, 2010
Plain meaning: Scientists who study running mice wrote that in animals, almost all thinking and learning is tied to moving around.
Who: German neuroscientist at the Center for Regenerative Therapies Dresden and the German Center for Neurodegenerative Diseases, Dresden; co-author of the 1999 study that found more new brain cells in running mice.
Source: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2010.00189/full (primary: open-access full text. The researcher read the journal page and the PubMed Central XML; the checker matched all three sentences in the Europe PMC full-text copy.)
Context: A review by the Dresden group on why running makes new nerve cells in the hippocampus of mice and rats. They argue that for an animal, learning and moving are one thing: it must move to find food, shelter and mates, so the brain treats movement as the sign that learning will be needed. The abstract adds: "In the wild (other than in front of a TV), no separation of physical and cognitive activity occurs."
Cautions: A hypothesis paper built on rodent studies: it says "In animals", "We argue" and "If further confirmed". The authors themselves write that adult neurogenesis "obviously could not be studied in humans". Do not present it as proven in people. The [...] marks show two cuts; each sentence is exact.
Last check: None in the text. This is a review article arguing a view ("We argue", "If further confirmed"), about animals (mice and rats), with no measured number. Keep the words "In animals" and do not turn it into a promise for people. The three-sentence version is joined with [...] from two places in the paper; the one-sentence cut is safer. Plain meaning tightened to match the one-sentence cut.

## 6. John J. Ratey, MD (with Eric Hagerman) · big word BUILD · science · quote
Quote: "Building muscles and conditioning the heart and lungs are essentially side effects. I often tell my patients that the point of exercise is to build and condition the brain."
Credit: John J. Ratey, MD, with Eric Hagerman · Spark: The Revolutionary New Science of Exercise and the Brain, 2008 (Introduction)
Plain meaning: A doctor says he tells his patients that stronger muscles and a fitter heart are side effects, and that the point of exercise is to build up the brain.
Who: American psychiatrist; the book's author note calls him an associate clinical professor of psychiatry at Harvard Medical School. He wrote the book with science writer Eric Hagerman.
Source: https://archive.org/details/sparkrevolutiona0000rate (primary, snippet view: the book's own scanned text in two archive.org scans of the 2008 first edition (sparkrevolutiona0000rate and sparkrevolutiona00rate), read by the researcher and again by the checker through overlapping full-text search snippets. Every word of both sentences was seen in both scans. Lending-only, so no page image was opened; the page number comes from the Contents and the scan's page map.)
Context: Opening paragraph of the book. Ratey says people think exercise feels good because of stress relief or endorphins, but "the real reason we feel so good when we get our blood pumping is that it makes the brain function at its best". Other lines seen in the same scans: (ch. 2, "Learning: Grow Your Brain Cells") "The body was designed to be pushed, and in pushing our bodies we push our brains too."; (Introduction) "our thinking brain evolved from the need to hone motor skills"; (ch. 10, "The Regimen: Build Your Brain", which starts on p. 245) "exercise is the single most powerful tool you have to optimize your brain function".
Cautions: A doctor's opinion in a popular book, not a measured finding. The same book makes treatment claims about ADHD, depression and addiction: do not pull those. Its epigraph signed "Plato" is not Plato's wording. Co-written with Hagerman, so credit "John J. Ratey, Spark (2008)". Living author. Page number 3 is by Contents and position, not by page image.
Last check: Words confirmed in the scan's own text, but the scan is lending-only so the PAGE IMAGE was not opened. Page 3 rests on the contents line and the page map (Introduction starts p. 3; the line is about 90 words in). Safer to credit 'Introduction' with no page number. It is the author's opinion ('in my view', 'I often tell my patients'), not a study result: do not present it as proven fact. Publisher's site today says 'clinical professor'; the 2008 book says 'associate clinical professor': on a slide use 'psychiatrist, Harvard Medical School'. Plain line tightened ('the point', not 'the main point'; heart added).

## 7. Sir Charles Sherrington · big word MUSCLE · science · quote
Quote: "As we look along the scale of life, whether in time or in order of organization, muscle is there before nerve, and nerve is there before mind, 'recognizable mind'."
Credit: Sir Charles Sherrington · Man on his Nature, 1940 (Gifford Lectures 1937-8), ch. VI, pp. 196-197
Plain meaning: Looking at living things from simple to complex, a Nobel Prize scientist saw that muscle appeared first, then nerves, then mind.
Who: English physiologist (1857-1952), Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 1932 for work on nerve cells and reflexes.
Source: https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.188837 (primary: scan of the 1940 first edition, text and page images checked by the researcher and again by the checker (p. 196 is scan leaf 214, p. 197 is leaf 215, p. 213 is leaf 231))
Context: Sherrington looks across living things from simple to complex and notes the order in which things appear: first muscle, then nerve, then mind. "Conative motor act" means a movement with a purpose. On p. 213 he says it again: "Mind, recognizable mind, seems to have arisen in connection with the motor act. Where motor integration progressed and where motor behaviour progressively evolved, mind progressively evolved."
Cautions: About evolution over ages, not about a person exercising. He writes with care ("It would seem"). "Conative" and "recognizable mind" need a gloss. The book is a set of lectures on natural theology, but this passage is plain biology. Do not swap in the famous "Life's aim is an act, not a thought": that line is not in this book and the researcher could not open its source.
Last check: None failed. Exact, including the second sentence ("It would seem to be the conative motor act under 'urge-to-live' which has been the cradle of mind.") if the longer form is wanted. Not in used.txt.

## 8. Pyotr Lesgaft (П. Ф. Лесгафт) · big word GROWTH · russia · quote
Quote: "Mental growth and development require a corresponding physical development."
Original: Умственный рост и развитие требуют соответствующего развития физического.
Credit: Pyotr Lesgaft (П. Ф. Лесгафт) · Guide to the Physical Education of School-Age Children, Part I, ch. II (1888; text of the 2nd edition, 1904) · researcher's translation
Plain meaning: A Russian doctor and teacher wrote that for the mind to grow, the body has to grow along with it.
Who: Russian anatomist, doctor and teacher (1837-1909), founder of scientific physical education in Russia.
Source: http://elib.gnpbu.ru/text/lesgaft_sps-t1_rukovodstvo_1951/ ; https://archive.org/details/ru_education_history_x1 (primary text in a scholarly reprint: page image of p. 287 of the 1951 collected edition seen (state pedagogical library scan); it reprints the 2nd edition, with Lesgaft's preface of 17 February 1904. The 1888 first edition was not opened, so its page is not known.)
Context: These are the first sentences of the chapter in which Lesgaft sets out what physical education in school is for. Two pages later (p. 289) he says that a developed mind in a very weak body "does not go unpunished": «мысль и понимание могут быть, но не будет надлежащей энергии для последовательной проверки идей и настойчивого проведения и применения их на практике» (thought and understanding may be there, but not the energy to test ideas and carry them through).
Cautions: Checked in the 1951 reprint of the 1904 second edition, not in the 1888 first edition. 19th-century anatomy: present as his view. The popular line «…умственное и физическое воспитание шли параллельно…» was not found in this chapter. He was dismissed from posts by the tsarist authorities but was not a politician.
Last check: Russian exact on the page image; English translation is faithful (it is the researcher's, so label it). The 1888 first edition itself was not seen: the 1951 volume prints the 2nd edition text (its 1904 preface says that, apart from added chapters, the edition is "only corrected"), so date it "1888; 2nd ed. 1904". The Egorov reader (1986) does contain the sentence (full-text hit on archive.org) but its page numbers 315-317 were not re-checked, so they are left out of the credit. The longer two-sentence version is also exact if wanted.

## 9. Nikolai A. Bernstein (Николай Александрович Бернштейн; spelled "Nicholai A. Bernstein" on the 1996 English title page) · big word NEED · russia · quote
Quote: "Motor tasks (motor needs) and the implacable necessity to move more quickly, accurately, and dexterously were the leading causes for the development of the brain and its accessories during all the millenia of evolution."
Original: Именно двигательные задачи, двигательные потребности, неумолимая жизненная необходимость двигаться все проворнее, все точнее, все ловче — вот что было ведущим началом в развитии мозга и всех его вспомогательных органов почти на всем протяжении его многовековой эволюции.
Credit: Nikolai A. Bernstein · On Dexterity and Its Development (written late 1940s, first printed 1991) · tr. Mark L. Latash, 1996
Plain meaning: A Russian scientist of movement wrote that the brain developed over a very long time mainly because animals had to move faster, more exactly and more skilfully.
Who: Soviet physiologist of movement (1896-1966), one of the founders of modern motor control science.
Source: http://elib.old.gnpbu.ru/text/bernshteyn_o-lovkosti_1991/ (primary: Russian read on the page images of the 1991 edition (PDF scan of the Ushinsky state pedagogical library, pp. 126-127) and in its HTML text. Latash's English read through archive.org full-text search inside the 1996 book (https://archive.org/details/dexterityitsdeve0000unse, lending copy, stitched from snippets) and through Google Books search inside the 2014 Psychology Press reissue; p. 104 fixed by the running heads and by Google Books.)
Context: A popular book on how movement skill is built. Here Bernstein explains why the brain grew new layers over evolution: animals kept facing harder movement tasks. Literal reading of the Russian: "It was precisely motor tasks, motor needs, the implacable vital necessity to move ever more nimbly, ever more precisely, ever more dexterously, that was the leading principle in the development of the brain and all its auxiliary organs through almost the whole of its age-long evolution." Same author, in the same Russian book (Очерк VI): «правильно проводимое упражнение есть, в сущности, повторение без повторения» ("exercise done rightly is, in essence, repetition without repetition").
Cautions: About animal evolution, not exercise training. In the next sentences Bernstein adds that in humans, very recently, movement stepped back behind mental and working needs, and he calls the human brain an "absolute dictator" of movement (a figure of speech): cut before that. Latash's English says "during all the millenia" (spelling as printed) where the Russian says "almost all": for Russian slides use the Russian; in English print Latash as it stands and never add words inside the quotation marks. In the Russian, «двигательные задачи» and «все проворнее, все точнее, все ловче» are in italics. The book was blocked from print in a political campaign of the late 1940s; its preface tells that story and names a state prize of that time, and a footnote elsewhere quotes Engels. Use only this one sentence and keep the bio short.
Last check: Both languages exact. Notes: (1) The English book prints 'millenia' (one n); that is the exact text, the normal spelling is 'millennia': owner's choice whether to print the book's spelling. (2) English says 'during all'; the Russian says 'почти на всем протяжении' (almost the whole): for Russian posts use the Russian line. (3) It is about evolution of the brain in animals, not a child's or adult's brain; the next sentence says that in humans movement has lately moved to second place behind mental and work needs. Do not caption it as 'exercise grows your brain'. (4) The candidate's slide cut stopped at '...development of the brain…', which hides that it is about evolution: print the full sentence (33 words). (5) Plain line changed: 'leading causes' is 'mainly because', not 'because'. (6) Do not mention the Stalin-era ban from the book's foreword on a slide.

## 10. John Dewey · big word EXERCISE · educator · quote
Quote: "nature's "intention" ... is to develop the mind especially by exercise of the muscles of the body"
Credit: John Dewey · Democracy and Education, 1916, ch. 9, p. 135
Plain meaning: Dewey said nature does not train the body first and the mind later: it grows the mind above all by using the muscles.
Who: American philosopher and educator (1859-1952), the best-known voice for learning by doing.
Source: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/852/pg852.txt | 1916 scan: https://archive.org/details/democracyeducati00deweuoft (primary (full text read; printed page 135 of the 1916 edition checked on the scan))
Context: Dewey is weighing Rousseau's rule "follow nature"; "he" is Rousseau. Dewey corrects him: the mind is not trained after the body but through it. He goes on: "the aim of following nature means, in the concrete, regard for the actual part played by use of the bodily organs in explorations, in handling of materials, in plays and games." ALSO VERIFIED in ch. 11, section 1: "For the pupil has a body, and brings it to school along with his mind. And the body is, of necessity, a wellspring of energy; it has to do something." and "Before the child goes to school, he learns with his hand, eye, and ear, because they are organs of the process of doing something from which meaning results."
Cautions: The sentence is conditional ("if he had said... he would have stated a positive fact"), so the slide cut needs the three dots and the full line in the caption. The 1916 first printing misprints "But if had said"; later copies and Project Gutenberg have "But if he had said". Dewey means purposeful doing (exploring, handling things, play, games); in ch. 11 he attacks mechanical muscle drill, so do not use him to praise repetition for its own sake. Another Dewey line from ch. 11 ("An ounce of experience...") was used in an earlier set.
Last check: Corrected one word. The candidate's long quote reads "But if he had said"; the printed book reads "But if had said". The word "he" is missing on p. 135 of the 1916 first printing and of the 1919, 1923 and 1928 Macmillan printings I checked; "he" is Project Gutenberg's silent fix. The final cut avoids that spot and is exact in every printing. If the full sentence is ever printed, write "But if [he] had said". "by" is in italics in the book. Plain meaning: added "above all" for Dewey's "especially". "he" and "his" are Rousseau; Dewey gives this as his own view ("a positive fact"). Not in used.txt (a different Dewey line was used before).

## 12. Plato · big word MOTION · philosopher · quote
Quote: "Let us assume, then, as a first principle in relation both to the body and soul of very young creatures, that nursing and moving about by day and night is good for them all"
Original: λάβωμεν τοίνυν τοῦτο οἷον στοιχεῖον ἐπʼ ἀμφότερα, σώματός τε καὶ ψυχῆς τῶν πάνυ νέων τὴν τιθήνησιν καὶ κίνησιν γιγνομένην ὅτι μάλιστα διὰ πάσης τε νυκτὸς καὶ ἡμέρας, ὡς ἔστι σύμφορος ἅπασι μέν

## S4. Zhu Xi (朱熹) · big word NURTURE · china · retold
Quote: "The ancients' elementary learning taught them through practical tasks, and that in itself nurtured their minds; without knowing or noticing it, they became good."
Original: 古人小學教之以事，便自養得他心，不知不覺自好了。
Credit: Zhu Xi (朱熹, 1130-1200) · 朱子語類 (Classified Conversations of Master Zhu), 卷七 · our translation
Plain meaning: Zhu Xi said that in old times children were first taught things to do, like rites, music, archery and arithmetic, and that this practice shaped their minds before they were taught the reasons.
Who: Chinese philosopher of the Song dynasty (1130-1200), the main voice of Neo-Confucian learning.
Source: https://archive.org/details/06049691.cn (Siku Quanshu scan, 卷五~卷七) ; https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/朱子語類/007 ; https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/周禮/地官司徒 (primary: both passages read on page images of the Siku Quanshu edition (archive.org 06049691.cn, images 136 and 139) and in the Chinese Wikisource transcription; they agree. The Zhouli lines were read as a transcription only.)
Context: Zhu Xi explains the old two-step schooling to his students. Children first learned by doing, in the six arts, which include archery and chariot driving beside writing and arithmetic; the study of principles came only at sixteen or seventeen. He says the doing itself formed their minds.
Cautions: Recorded sayings, not his own pen. "事" also covers household duties and manners, not only physical arts, so the line is about doing before theory. Zhu Xi also taught quiet sitting, and Yan Yuan attacks him for it, so he is no champion of exercise. He was a scholar-official, not a minister. The Zhouli line on 保氏 opens with admonishing the king: do not quote that part. Used before with another line (used.txt 157).
Last check: Corrected the translation wording and the plain meaning. Chinese exact in the Siku scan; both recorder names (驤, 賀孫) confirmed. The English is our own translation and must be labelled so. I changed "taught them through doing things" to "taught them through practical tasks": 事 means affairs or tasks, and "doing" was added by the translator. In the longer line, "was taught only by doing things" should likewise read "was taught only practical things". These are sayings written down by his students (compiled 1270). Theme caution: he says practice first, theory later; archery and chariot driving are two of the six arts beside writing and arithmetic, so do not caption it as a line about exercise or the body.
