The Five Kleshas — The Roots of Suffering

五种烦恼 · 苦的根源 — for Indepth China
~18–20 minutes · 9 slides · Companion to index.html · Ember for the affliction, gold for the light.

How to hold this one

This is a heavier register than the hormone talk — you are naming suffering. Slow down. Let the stories breathe; the myths carry the weight, you don't have to push them. Stay in ember gravity through the five afflictions (slides 3–7); when you reach the four states and "messengers" (slide 8), let the gold — the light — enter your voice. Close in silence.

One honesty note that raises your authority: the rope-snake and the tiger-among-goats are Vedanta teaching parables, not verses of the Yoga Sutras — so say "there's a story the old teachers tell." Yayāti, Śiśupāla, Nachiketā and Rāvaṇa are canonical (Mahābhārata, Kaṭha Upaniṣad, Rāmāyaṇa) — you can name the source.

Pace: ~2 min per klesha, 1 min on the frame/close. Total ≈ 18–20 min, leaving room for their questions.
01

Title

0:00
Say
"For years you have worked with pain on the mat. Tonight we look straight at where it comes from — the five afflictions the sutras call the kleshas, the roots of suffering. And Patanjali makes a promise: heyaṃ duḥkham anāgatam — the suffering that hasn't come yet can be avoided. That's why we study this."
02

The root & the chain

1:00
Say
"Pain in practice is not just physical. Watch the chain: knee pain in lotus → frustration → the breath tightens → and the pain grows. The body started it; the mind multiplied it. And underneath all of it sits one root — avidyā, ignorance. Patanjali calls it the field: the other four afflictions are not separate weeds, they are the crop that grows from this one soil."
Ask
"So before we name them — which of your pains this week was the knee… and which was the mind?"
03

Avidyā अविद्या · 无明

2:00
Say
"Avidyā — ignorance. Not lack of information; a misreading of reality. Mistaking the impermanent for the permanent, and the not-Self for the Self. It is the root of the other four."
Story
There's a story the old teachers tell. A tiger cub, orphaned, is raised by goats. It learns to bleat, to nibble grass, to be afraid — certain it is a weak little goat. One day a great tiger seizes it, drags it to a still pool, and shows it its own reflection — then makes it taste meat. And the cub, for the first time, roars.
— Vedānta teaching parable (Ramakrishna tradition). Say "a story teachers tell," not "the sutra says."
Turns sideways
It lived its whole life weak and afraid — not from any real limit, but from a false name it had been given for itself. That is avidyā: bondage is a case of mistaken identity.
Antidote
Svādhyāya (self-study), presence, and viveka — discernment. Insight, not force: when the light comes, the "snake" was always only a rope.
04

Asmitā अस्मिता · 我执

4:00
Say
"From that root grows the first shoot — asmitā, egoism. The moment the mind says 'I am this.' Patanjali defines it exactly: confusing the Seer with the instrument of seeing. The subtler you get, the stickier it becomes — 'I am the one who has transcended ego' is still ego."
Story
Rāvaṇa — ten-headed king of Lanka, a great scholar and Shiva-devotee. When he won his boon of invulnerability, he asked to be safe from gods and demons — but so proud, he disdained even to name humans and animals as possible threats. His ego wouldn't stoop to it. And that overlooked loophole is exactly what killed him: a "mere human," Rāma, brought him down.
— Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa. (The "ten heads = ten vices" reading is a later devotional gloss.)
Turns sideways
It was not his power that authored his ruin. It was his ego. Feedback he could have used, he received as insult — and became uncoachable.
Antidote
Humility in repetition. Gratitude in surrender. The mat humbles the "I" six days a week, if you let it.
05

Rāga राग · 贪

6:00
Say
"The ego reaches toward what flatters it — that's rāga, attachment. Clinging to pleasure, and organizing your life around repeating it."
Story
King Yayāti, cursed with old age while his appetite still burned, begged his sons for their youth; only the youngest, Puru, agreed. Yayāti took it and indulged the senses for a thousand more years — and at the end he confessed the discovery: desire is never quenched by enjoyment. It flares higher — like a fire fed with ghee. He returned Puru's youth and renounced.
— Mahābhārata, Ādi Parva. na jātu kāmaḥ kāmānām upabhogena śāmyati.
Turns sideways
A whole extra millennium of pleasure left him emptier than when he began. Craving is a treadmill: each hit resets the baseline, and satisfaction is always one more away.
Antidote
Remember impermanence. Enjoy what comes; let go when it goes. Don't hunt yesterday's practice.
06

Dveṣa द्वेष · 嗔

8:30
Say
"The mirror image of craving is dveṣa, aversion — recoiling from what once brought pain. Grudge, avoidance, revulsion. The trap: aversion keeps the disliked thing glued to your attention. You think about the enemy more than the friend."
Story
Śiśupāla spent his whole life hating Krishna — allied against him, insulted him at every turn. Krishna had vowed to forgive one hundred offenses. At Yudhiṣṭhira's great sacrifice, enraged that Krishna was honored first, Śiśupāla poured out abuse and crossed the hundredth insult — and the Sudarśana-chakra took his head on the spot.
— Mahābhārata, Sabhā Parva (Śiśupāla-vadha).
Turns sideways
His aversion drove him to publicly provoke the very being he hated most — delivering him straight into the ruin he meant to inflict. Aversion eats the one who holds it.
Antidote
Steady breath through the challenge. Meet aversion with its opposite. The backbend you avoid becomes the doorway — dveṣa becomes the door.
07

Abhiniveśā अभिनिवेश · 怖畏

11:00
Say
"And the deepest — abhiniveśā, the clinging to life, the fear of death. Patanjali says it grips even the wise. Generalized, it's every smaller fear: of losing the youth, the status, the progress, the identity. And its cruel signature is that the grip causes the very loss it fears."
Story
The boy Nachiketā, sent by his father's rash word to the house of Death, waited three nights at Yama's door. Granted three boons, for the last he asked the ultimate question: what survives death? Yama tried to divert him — offered sons, cattle, gold, vast lands, celestial dancing-girls, long life — the very bribes that are abhiniveśā. Nachiketā refused them all: "these wear out the senses; keep your horses and your dancers." And by facing death and refusing the clinging, he won the teaching of the deathless Self.
— Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1–2.
Turns sideways
The counter-image: Aesop's dog, crossing a stream with a bone, sees its own reflection, lunges to seize that "other" bone too — and drops the real one into the water. Grasping for more security, it loses what it had.
Antidote
You are not the body. Follow the task at hand. One step at a time. Fear of death is beaten not by escaping death, but by knowing what in you death cannot touch.
08

The four states · messengers

14:00
Say — light enters here
"One caution, especially for you, because you're advanced: a klesha you can't feel is not a klesha you've killed. Patanjali names four states — dormant, attenuated, interrupted, active. A dormant seed is not a dead seed; it waits for its conditions. So maturity is not 'I have no ego' — it's knowing the seed is still in the soil."
Ask
"Which klesha is running you right now — and is it active… or just dormant, waiting?"
Say
"And here is the turn: the kleshas are not enemies. They are messengers. Craving shows you where you've outsourced your peace. Aversion maps the places you refuse to feel. Fear points straight at what you're still identified with. You don't declare war on a messenger — you read the message. The tools are insight, not force: kriyā yoga (discipline, self-study, surrender), pratipakṣa bhāvana (cultivate the opposite), and viveka (discernment)."
09

Close

17:00
Say — slower
"So don't push the affliction away. The knee pain, the craving, the grudge, the fear — they are not interruptions to the work. They are the work, arriving right on time to show you where you are still asleep."
— pause, one full breath —
"The klesha is not the wall in your practice. It is the doorway — and each one is carved with your own name."

If a student pushes back — honest answers

Naming the limit precisely is what authority sounds like. Never bluff.

"Is the tiger-and-goats story in the Yoga Sutras?"
No — it's a Vedanta teaching parable (Ramakrishna tradition) paired with avidyā. The sutra itself just defines avidyā (2.5). Same for the rope-snake.
"Are the others real scripture?"
Yes. Yayāti = Mahābhārata (Ādi Parva); Śiśupāla = Mahābhārata (Sabhā Parva); Nachiketā = Kaṭha Upaniṣad; Rāvaṇa = Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa.
"If I don't feel a klesha, is it gone?"
Not necessarily. YS 2.4 lists four states — dormant, attenuated, interrupted, active. Absence of a flare-up is not freedom; the seed can still be in the soil.
"Why is avidyā the 'root'?"
YS 2.4 — avidyā is the field (kṣetram). Because you misperceive a passing self as permanent, ego forms to defend it, then reaches (rāga), recoils (dveṣa), and clings in fear (abhiniveśā). Pull the root, the plant loses its ground.
