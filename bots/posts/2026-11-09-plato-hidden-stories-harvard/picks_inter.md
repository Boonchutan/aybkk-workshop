# Picks: Hidden Stories, Intermediate Series (skeptic pass, 9 Nov 2026)

New stories get no number ("Ep. ?"). Research only. No script yet.

## Step 1. Are the asanas really in Intermediate?

All four are confirmed.

  https://ayi.info/fileadmin/01_Ashtanga/%C3%9Cbungsserien/Cheat_Sheet_EN/06_IntermediateSeries-EN.pdf

  Sheet 2 starts "Vātāyanāsana, Parighāsana, Gomukhāsana A + B, Supta Ūrdhva Pāda Vajrāsana".
- Independent: Kino MacGregor's blog (https://kinoyoga.com/?p=14572) says "Day 18 of #TheWorkOfSecondSeries

- Independent: Wikipedia "Ashtanga (vinyasa) yoga" cites Nancy Gilgoff. It puts Karandavasana, Parighasana and
  Gomukhasana in Intermediate, but it does not name Nakrasana or Vatayanasana.
- Vatayanasana: a David Garrigues class page calls it a Second Series asana. I saw this only in search results
  and did not open the page. The AYI sheet is the checked source.

Name meanings, checked by me in Monier-Williams (Cologne, 2020 web edition):
- nakra m. "crocodile, alligator" (p. 524).
- vātāyana m. "'moving or fleet as w[ind]', a horse" (p. 935). Tagged "L." (word lists only). The neuter word
  means "a window". So say "horse, fast as the wind". Never say "horse face".
- mayūra "a peacock" (p. 789). piñcha "a wing (= piccha)" (p. 624).

## PICK 1. The Rock That Answered (Nakrasana). Lane B, comment aybkk

Why: this is the best fit for "same practice, same time, a teacher who notices". The monkey stays alive BECAUSE
his road never changes and he checks it every day (Harvard #27). The twist, a crocodile answering for a rock, is
funny. A 5-year-old gets it, and it resets attention inside the story. Nobody dies. The asana link is real:
nakra = crocodile. And "the rock stood higher" matches hips that pop up in Nakrasana, which a teacher sees.
This telling is much less known than the heart-in-the-fig-tree one (Jataka 208, Panchatantra Book 4).
Harvard tricks: #1 fork, #27 because, #21, #30.

Verified (Vanarinda Jataka No. 57, Cowell ed. vol. I, tr. R. Chalmers 1895, pp. 142-143, Fausbøll pp. [278]-[280]):
- The monkey is "as big as a mare's foal and enormously strong". He lives alone on a river bank. On the island
  in the river there are mangoes and bread-fruits, and "a solitary rock" stands halfway [278].
- He leaps from bank to rock to island, eats, and returns "at evening by the way he came. And such was his life
  from day to day." [278]
- The crocodile "took his stand on the rock, meaning to catch the monkey on his evening journey home" [279].
- "the Bodhisatta always marked the exact height of the water in the river, and of the rock in the water ...
  though the water stood at the same level, the rock seemed to stand higher" [279]
- He calls "Hi! rock!" once, then three times. Then: "How comes it, friend rock, that you won't answer me
  to-day?" (p. 143)
- The crocodile thinks: "so the rock's in the habit of answering the monkey. I must answer for the rock to-day."
  He calls back: "Yes, monkey; what is it?" (p. 143)
- "As there was no other way back" ... "Open your mouth and catch me when I jump." (p. 143)
- "when crocodiles open their mouths, their eyes shut". Translator's footnote 1: "This assertion is not in
  accord with the facts of natural history." (p. 143)
- "made a jump on to the crocodile's head, and thence, with a spring like lightning, gained the bank." (p. 143)
- The crocodile praises him: "Truth, foresight, fixed resolve, and fearlessness" [280]. Then he goes home.
  No one is hurt.
- The frame: Devadatta's "murderous intent". Devadatta is the crocodile and Cinca is his wife. Leave it out.
- The translator's note says "Cf. No. 224 (Kumbhila)", which in vol. II p. 145 has the verses only. It also
  points to a Chinese version (Beal) and a Japanese one (Griffin). I did not open those two.

Do NOT say:
- "to eat your heart", the pregnant wife, Devadatta, Cinca, or "murder".
- "crocodiles close their eyes" as a fact. Say only "his eyes closed".
- That this version is unknown in China or Japan.
- "danger" in a way that hints at injury. No promise that the same practice prevents injury.
- Any joke about the monkey. He is the Bodhisatta, so tell him kindly. Do not name him "monkey king".
- That the viewer is "bad" like the crocodile. The link is how flat and low the crocodile lay.

Beats (H, DH, T1, RH, T2, P):
1. H (bank #10, flipped to contrarian): "Same practice every morning is boring? Boring is how you notice."
2. DH (open loop): "One old story. A monkey stay alive because his road never change."
3. T1 (FORK): Bedtime story for my son. Big monkey jumps bank, rock, island every day and comes home the
   same way. He always looks at the water and the rock. One evening the water is the same, but the rock is
   higher. Jump or not? What you do? (#1)
4. RH (TWIST): He calls "Hello, rock!" three times. "Rock, why you don't answer me today?" The crocodile on
   the rock thinks the rock always talks. So he answers, "Yes, monkey?"
5. T2 (worst moment): Now he knows. A crocodile waits to catch him. No other way home.
6. P: "Open your mouth, catch me when I jump." The mouth opens, the eyes close. He jumps on the head and onto
   the bank. Same in your practice: the same asanas every day are your water mark. In Nakrasana you lie like
   that crocodile, flat and low. Hips go up, teacher see, like the monkey saw the rock.
   [BOONCHU'S OWN CUE: 5 words or less]. Close: "If your practice change every day, how you know what changed?"

## PICK 2. The Horses That Looked Weak (Vatayanasana). Lane B, comment aybkk

Why: the story pattern is clean. Fail: the horses fall to their knees. Hero: calm hands lift them. Payoff: they
run like the wind. The asana name matches this scene's own words: Ganguli has "fleet as the winds" and "speed of
the wind", and MW gives vātāyana = a horse, fleet as wind. The horses go down on their knees and rise, just as
Vatayanasana takes one knee down and then you rise. The story answers the top local fear, "too stiff, too weak"
(hook bank #42 and #102), and shows a teacher who knows the marks. It is rarely told, even by people who know
Nala and Damayanti. Harvard tricks: #1 fork, #27 (the marks are the because), #20, #30.

Verified (Mahabharata, Vana Parva, Nalopakhyana, tr. K.M. Ganguli, Project Gutenberg #15474):
- Sec. LXVII: "My name is Vahuka. There is no one in this world equal to me in managing steeds." He is made the
  head of the stables.
- Sec. LXXI: "I will go to the city of the Vidarbhas in a single day."
- Sec. LXXI: he "selected some steeds that were lean-fleshed, yet strong and capable of a long journey ... born
  in (the country of) Sindhu, and fleet as the winds."
- Sec. LXXI: the master "said somewhat angrily ... Thou shouldst not jest with us. How can these horses of
  mine, weak in strength and breath, carry us? And how shall we be able to go this long way".
- Sec. LXXI: "Each of these horses bears one curl on his forehead, two on his temples, four on his sides, four
  on his chest, and one on his back." He yokes "four excellent steeds".
- Sec. LXXI: "the king ... mounted upon the car, when those best of horses fell down upon the ground on their
  knees." Then he "began to soothe" them, and "raising them up with the reins" they "rose to the sky",
  "steeds gifted with the speed of the wind".
- Sec. LXXII: "Like a bird coursing through the sky, Nala soon crossed rivers and mountains". The master's upper
  garment drops. He asks to stop, and the answer is: "The sheet is dropped down far away. We have travelled one
  yojana thence. Therefore, it is incapable of being recovered."
- The hidden context, all of it to leave out: Vahuka is King Nala in disguise, "of unsightly appearance"
  (LXXI). The rider is King Rituparna of Ayodhya. The rush is for Damayanti's second choosing. LXXII goes on to
  a leaf-counting and dice-science scene.

Do NOT say:
- "He did not whip them." The text never says so. It says he soothed them and lifted them with the reins.
- Why they fell ("too heavy", "first load"). The text gives no reason.
- How many curls. The same paragraph says "ten hairy curls" but lists twelve.
- "Nala". It is also the name in the bridge story already used. Do not say "hidden king", "king", "throne",
  "Ayodhya" or "Damayanti". Say "a man" and "his new horse man". If a name is used, explain it.
- "ugly" or any joke about looks. Say "he did not look special".
- The yojana in km. Do not say "the horses flew". Say "like a bird in the sky" (the text).
- That Vatayanasana means "horse face" or "window".
- A breath or health promise to the viewer.

Beats (H, DH, T1, RH, T2, P):
1. H (bank #102 / #42, flipped to contrarian): "You think you too weak to start? Best horses in this story look
   weak."
2. DH (open loop): "And first time, they fall down on their knees."
3. T1 (FORK): Bedtime story for my son. A man must reach a far city in one day. His new horse man picks four
   thin horses. The man is angry: "Don't joke with me. Weak in strength and breath." The horse man shows small
   curls of hair, good marks. Thin horses, long road, one day. You pick them? What you do? (#1)
4. RH (TWIST): The man says OK and climbs in. And the horses fall down on their knees.
5. T2 (worst moment): A long road and one day only, with the horses on the ground. Everyone thinks: too weak.
6. P: He calms them. He lifts them with the reins. They stand up. They run like the wind, like a bird in the
   sky. The man's shawl falls off, and it is already far behind. Same for you: the first week, you fall on your
   knees too. Vatayanasana means horse, fast as the wind. Knee goes down, then you rise.
   [BOONCHU'S OWN CUE: 5 words or less]. Close: "Who decide you too weak? You, or someone who know the marks?"

## Not picked

- inter-A-peacock-forgot-once (Pincha Mayurasana): the facts are correct (T982 418a-b and 419a; Mora Jataka
  No. 159, Cowell II pp. 23-26). I hold it as a reserve only. The Mora Jataka was dropped once before (3 Oct)
  for kings. The best twist (seven years, the trained peahen calling before the words) exists only in the Pali,
  which also has six kings, deaths, and a frame that blames "womankind". The clean Chinese telling lacks that
  twist, so using both means blending. In both texts the trap is desire ("woke desire"; 貪欲愛著).
  T982 then tells you to say the words against poison, snakes and "404 diseases", so the link drifts toward a
  protection promise. The Mora verse is a living Thai protective chant, and Thai viewers (Boonchu too) may know
  the peahen story; my search did not confirm this. The asana link is the name only.
- inter-D-foal-on-lotus-leaves (Vatayanasana): KILLED. The quote is misattributed: "whether he knew his own power
  or no" is a food test (he refuses rice-scum, then says "I am chief of steeds", Cowell II p. 202). It is not the
  running. The running and the lotus leaves are a show for the king ("See my horse's speed, O king!", p. 202),
  and it ends with "the half of his kingdom" and "lordship over all India" (pp. 202-203). Royalty is the stage.
  It also overlaps Pick 2, and its Habit Gap link is weak.
