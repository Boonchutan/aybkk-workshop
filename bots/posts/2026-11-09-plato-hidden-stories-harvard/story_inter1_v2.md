Answer: yes, real (Vanarinda Jataka No. 57, Cowell/Chalmers vol. I pp. 142-143). v1 had cut the danger (the wife wants the monkey's heart, "To catch you and eat your heart"). v2 puts it back.

Toolkit pick: /hook contrarian (bank #10 flipped) + Fork-First Story. Harvard #1 (line 11 fork), #27 (line 20 because), #21 (line 21), #30 (one ask in caption).
Stakes: if your practice changes every day, you can't see a small change, and neither can your teacher. Why care: you think same practice is boring.

Source lines (Cowell vol. I, local copy wk6/HS/dl_inter, lines 9568-9617): mate "conceive a longing for the monkey's heart to eat. So she begged her lord to catch the monkey"; "took his stand on the rock, meaning to catch the monkey on his evening journey home"; "always marked the exact height of the water in the river, and of the rock"; "though the water stood at the same level, the rock seemed to stand higher"; "Hi! rock!" three times; "How comes it, friend rock, that you won't answer me to-day?"; "so the rock's in the habit of answering the monkey... Yes, monkey; what is it?"; "Who are you?" "I'm a crocodile." ... "To catch you and eat your heart."; "no other way back"; "There's no help for it then but to give myself up to you. Open your mouth and catch me when I jump."; "when crocodiles open their mouths, their eyes shut" (Cowell's footnote: not true in nature, so never say it as a fact about real crocodiles); "made a jump on to the crocodile's head, and thence, with a spring like lightning, gained the bank."
Left out: the king line, the frame (Devadatta), "monkey-king". In the book the monkey is the Bodhisatta (the Buddha in a past life): not said in the video; caption may say "an old Buddhist story".
