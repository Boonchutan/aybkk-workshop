## Big findings (read these first)

1. ARISTOTLE is the one ancient author who says "body first" in plain words, twice (Politics VII.15 and VIII.3). PLATO does not: in the Republic he starts with music and says the good soul improves the body, not the other way round (403d). Plato's real lines are "both together" (Timaeus) and "movement from birth" (Laws).
2. The English of the famous Aristotle line exists in two wordings. Jowett 1885: "habit must go before reason, and the body before the mind". Jowett revised by Ross 1921 (the one on most websites): "practice must be used before theory, and the body be trained before the mind". Both are real. Name the edition.
3. The two "Plato exercise quotes" that float online are not Plato's words. Real passages found (see Dropped 1 and 2).
4. The "Cicero" gym quote ("It is exercise alone that supports the spirits, and keeps the mind in vigor") is a real 1744 translation line, but in Cicero it is about exercising the MIND and is contrasted with tiring the body. It says the opposite of how it is used (Dropped 4).

## The 10 candidates sent

### 1. aristotle-body-before-mind (theme fit 10)
Aristotle, Politics VIII.3, 1338b4-8. About 330 BC.
English, Jowett revised by W. D. Ross, 1921 (vol. X of the Oxford translation):
> Now it is clear that in education practice must be used before theory, and the body be trained before the mind; and therefore boys should be handed over to the trainer, who creates in them the proper habit of body, and to the wrestling-master, who teaches them their exercises.

Jowett's own first edition (Oxford 1885, vol. I, p. 248): "Now it is clear that in education habit must go before reason, and the body before the mind; and therefore boys should be handed over to the trainer, who creates in them the proper habit of body, and to the wrestling-master, who teaches them their exercises."
Greek (Ross, Oxford Classical Text 1957, via Perseus):
> ἐπεὶ δὲ φανερὸν πρότερον τοῖς ἔθεσιν ἢ τῷ λόγῳ παιδευτέον εἶναι, καὶ περὶ τὸ σῶμα πρότερον ἢ τὴν διάνοιαν, δῆλον ἐκ τούτων ὅτι παραδοτέον τοὺς παῖδας γυμναστικῇ καὶ παιδοτριβικῇ· τούτων γὰρ ἡ μὲν ποιάν τινα ποιεῖ τὴν ἕξιν τοῦ σώματος, ἡ δὲ τὰ ἔργα.

Context: Aristotle lists what children should learn (reading, writing, drawing, music, gymnastics) and fixes the order. The very next chapter (VIII.4) warns against hard training for children ("of a lighter kind, avoiding severe diet or painful toil, lest the growth of the body be impaired") and adds 1339a7-10: "Men ought not to labour at the same time with their minds and with their bodies; for the two kinds of labour are opposed to one another; the labour of the body impedes the mind, and the labour of the mind the body."
Slide cut: "in education practice must be used before theory, and the body be trained before the mind"
URLs: https://archive.org/details/worksofaristotle0000wdro_f0a6 ; https://archive.org/details/politicsofaristo01arisuoft ; http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.8.eight.html

### 2. aristotle-care-of-body-first (theme fit 9)
Aristotle, Politics VII.15, 1334b20-28. (Some editions number Book VII as Book IV; the Bekker numbers stay the same.)
English, Jowett revised by Ross, 1921:
> And as the body is prior in order of generation to the soul, so the irrational is prior to the rational. The proof is that anger and wishing and desire are implanted in children from their very birth, but reason and understanding are developed as they grow older. Wherefore, the care of the body ought to precede that of the soul, and the training of the appetitive part should follow: none the less our care of it must be for the sake of the reason, and our care of the body for the sake of the soul.

(1885 edition: same, except "anger and will and desire".)
Greek:
> ὥσπερ δὲ τὸ σῶμα πρότερον τῇ γενέσει τῆς ψυχῆς, οὕτω καὶ τὸ ἄλογον τοῦ λόγον ἔχοντος. φανερὸν δὲ καὶ τοῦτο· θυμὸς γὰρ καὶ βούλησις, ἔτι δὲ ἐπιθυμία, καὶ γενομένοις εὐθὺς ὑπάρχει τοῖς παιδίοις, ὁ δὲ λογισμὸς καὶ ὁ νοῦς προϊοῦσιν ἐγγίγνεσθαι πέφυκεν. διὸ πρῶτον μὲν τοῦ σώματος τὴν ἐπιμέλειαν ἀναγκαῖον εἶναι προτέραν ἢ τὴν τῆς ψυχῆς, ἔπειτα τὴν τῆς ὀρέξεως, ἕνεκα μέντοι τοῦ νοῦ τὴν τῆς ὀρέξεως, τὴν δὲ τοῦ σώματος τῆς ψυχῆς.

Context: Aristotle asks whether early training should start with reason or with habit. His answer: the body comes first in time, then desire, then reason; and the body is cared for "for the sake of the soul".
Slide cut: "the care of the body ought to precede that of the soul ... and our care of the body for the sake of the soul."
Caveat: "care of the body" (epimeleia) is wider than exercise (food, health). The next chapter (VII.16) goes on to marriage age and childbirth rules: leave it alone.

### 3. plato-laws-infants-motion (theme fit 9)
Plato, Laws VII, 790c-d. About 350 BC. Speaker: the Athenian Stranger, to Cleinias.
English, Jowett (3rd ed., Oxford 1892, vol. V, p. 170; Gutenberg #1750):
> Let us assume, then, as a first principle in relation both to the body and soul of very young creatures, that nursing and moving about by day and night is good for them all, and that the younger they are, the more they will need it; infants should live, if that were possible, as if they were always rocking at sea.

(The Gutenberg text prints Jowett's footnote "(compare Arist. Pol.)" inside the sentence after "need it". In the printed book it is a footnote, so it is left out here.)
Greek (Burnet, Platonis Opera vol. 5, Oxford Classical Text, via Perseus):
> λάβωμεν τοίνυν τοῦτο οἷον στοιχεῖον ἐπʼ ἀμφότερα, σώματός τε καὶ ψυχῆς τῶν πάνυ νέων τὴν τιθήνησιν καὶ κίνησιν γιγνομένην ὅτι μάλιστα διὰ πάσης τε νυκτὸς καὶ ἡμέρας, ὡς ἔστι σύμφορος ἅπασι μέν, οὐχ ἥκιστα δὲ τοῖς ὅτι νεωτάτοισι, καὶ οἰκεῖν, εἰ δυνατὸν ἦν, οἷον ἀεὶ πλέοντας·

Same passage, 789d: "all bodies are benefited by shakings and movements, when they are moved without weariness".
Context: Book VII opens the education of children with movement, starting before birth (the mother should walk) and in the first three years (nurses should carry the child about). Movement is called good for the child's body and soul. Plato's proof is that mothers rock restless babies to sleep, and that motion calms fear.
Slide cut: "nursing and moving about by day and night is good for them all, and that the younger they are, the more they will need it"
Caveats: it is passive movement (carrying, rocking), not exercise. The "soul" benefit named is calm and courage, not intelligence. The passage also uses fighting cocks and the Corybantic and Bacchic rites as examples: keep those out.

### 4. plato-timaeus-not-soul-without-body (theme fit 8)
Plato, Timaeus 88b-c. About 360 BC. Speaker: Timaeus (not Socrates).
English, Jowett (3rd ed. 1892, vol. III; Gutenberg #1572):
> There is one protection against both kinds of disproportion:—that we should not move the body without the soul or the soul without the body, and thus they will be on their guard against each other, and be healthy and well balanced. And therefore the mathematician or any one else whose thoughts are much absorbed in some intellectual pursuit, must allow his body also to have due exercise, and practise gymnastic; and he who is careful to fashion the body, should in turn impart to the soul its proper motions, and should cultivate music and all philosophy, if he would deserve to be called truly fair and truly good.

Greek (Burnet, Platonis Opera vol. 4, Oxford Classical Text, via Perseus):
> μία δὴ σωτηρία πρὸς ἄμφω, μήτε τὴν ψυχὴν ἄνευ σώματος κινεῖν μήτε σῶμα ἄνευ ψυχῆς, ἵνα ἀμυνομένω γίγνησθον ἰσορρόπω καὶ ὑγιῆ. τὸν δὴ μαθηματικὸν ἤ τινα ἄλλην σφόδρα μελέτην διανοίᾳ κατεργαζόμενον καὶ τὴν τοῦ σώματος ἀποδοτέον κίνησιν, γυμναστικῇ προσομιλοῦντα, τόν τε αὖ σῶμα ἐπιμελῶς πλάττοντα τὰς τῆς ψυχῆς ἀνταποδοτέον κινήσεις, μουσικῇ καὶ πάσῃ φιλοσοφίᾳ προσχρώμενον, εἰ μέλλει δικαίως τις ἅμα μὲν καλός, ἅμα δὲ ἀγαθὸς ὀρθῶς κεκλῆσθαι.

Context: Timaeus says the worst disproportion is between soul and body. A strong mind in a weak body wears it out; a strong body with a weak mind makes the soul "dull, and stupid, and forgetful". The cure is to exercise both. A few lines on (89a): "of all modes of purifying and re-uniting the body the best is gymnastic".
Slide cuts: "we should not move the body without the soul or the soul without the body" or "the mathematician or any one else whose thoughts are much absorbed in some intellectual pursuit, must allow his body also to have due exercise"
Caveats: "both together", not "body first". Jowett swaps the Greek order (Greek: "neither the soul without body nor body without soul").

### 5. plato-republic-gymnastic-for-the-soul (theme fit 7)
Plato, Republic III, 410b-c. About 375 BC. Socrates speaking with Glaucon.
English, Jowett (3rd ed. 1892, vol. III; Gutenberg #1497):
> Neither are the two arts of music and gymnastic really designed, as is often supposed, the one for the training of the soul, the other for the training of the body.
> What then is the real object of them?
> I believe, I said, that the teachers of both have in view chiefly the improvement of the soul.

Greek (Burnet, Platonis Opera vol. 4, Oxford Classical Text, via Perseus):
> ἆρʼ οὖν, ἦν δʼ ἐγώ, ὦ Γλαύκων, καὶ οἱ καθιστάντες μουσικῇ καὶ γυμναστικῇ παιδεύειν οὐχ οὗ ἕνεκά τινες οἴονται καθιστᾶσιν, ἵνα τῇ μὲν τὸ σῶμα θεραπεύοιντο, τῇ δὲ τὴν ψυχήν; ἀλλὰ τί μήν; ἔφη. κινδυνεύουσιν, ἦν δʼ ἐγώ, ἀμφότερα τῆς ψυχῆς ἕνεκα τὸ μέγιστον καθιστάναι.

Context: Socrates is planning the schooling of the city's guardians. "Music" means all the arts and letters. His point: physical training is not for muscles, it shapes character (the "spirited" part); training with no music makes a man "like a wild beast", music with no training makes him soft. 411e-412a sums up: "some God, as I should say, has given mankind two arts answering to them (and only indirectly to the soul and body), in order that these two principles (like the strings of an instrument) may be relaxed or drawn tighter until they are duly harmonized."
Slide cut: "the teachers of both have in view chiefly the improvement of the soul."
Caveats (a critic will find these): Republic II 376e: "Shall we begin education with music, and go on to gymnastic afterwards? By all means." Republic III 403d: "not that the good body by any bodily excellence improves the soul, but, on the contrary, that the good soul, by her own excellence, improves the body as far as this may be possible." So never caption this "Plato: body first".

### 6. xenophon-thinking-needs-the-body (theme fit 8)
Xenophon, Memorabilia III.12.5-6. About 370 BC. Socrates speaking to a young man, Epigenes, who is out of condition.
English, E. C. Marchant (Loeb Classical Library, 1923):
> For in everything that men do the body is useful; and in all uses of the body it is of great importance to be in as high a state of physical efficiency as possible. Why, even in the process of thinking, in which the use of the body seems to be reduced to a minimum, it is matter of common knowledge that grave mistakes may often be traced to bad health.

Greek (Marchant, Oxford 1921, via Perseus):
> πρὸς πάντα γὰρ ὅσα πράττουσιν ἄνθρωποι χρήσιμον τὸ σῶμά ἐστιν· ἐν πάσαις δὲ ταῖς τοῦ σώματος χρείαις πολὺ διαφέρει ὡς βέλτιστα τὸ σῶμα ἔχειν· ἐπεὶ καὶ ἐν ᾧ δοκεῖ ἐλαχίστη σώματος χρεία εἶναι, ἐν τῷ διανοεῖσθαι, τίς οὐκ οἶδεν ὅτι καὶ ἐν τούτῳ πολλοὶ μεγάλα σφάλλονται διὰ τὸ μὴ ὑγιαίνειν τὸ σῶμα;

Context: this is the same short chapter as the line used before ("a disgrace to grow old...", III.12.8), but a different sentence. Socrates tells Epigenes that a trained body helps in everything a person does, even in thinking.
Slide cut: "even in the process of thinking, in which the use of the body seems to be reduced to a minimum, ... grave mistakes may often be traced to bad health."
Caveats: the chapter starts from fitness for war (III.12.1-5): keep that out. The next sentence lists "loss of memory, depression, discontent, insanity" as coming from a body in bad condition: do NOT use it, it reads as a medical claim. These are Socrates' words as Xenophon reports them.

### 7. pliny-mind-stirred-by-movement (theme fit 9) SAFETY FLAG
Pliny the Younger, Letters I.6.2, to Tacitus. About AD 100.
English, William Melmoth, revised by F. C. T. Bosanquet (Harvard Classics vol. 9, 1909; Gutenberg #2811, where it is Letter IV):
> it is wonderful how the mind is stirred and quickened into activity by brisk bodily exercise.

Latin (Perseus):
> mirum est ut animus agitatione motuque corporis excitetur

More literal: J. B. Firth, 1900 (attalus.org, which says a few words were modified): "It is really surprising how the mind is stimulated by bodily movement and exercise."
Context: a joking letter. Pliny went boar hunting, sat by the nets with his writing tablets, and came home with three boars and full notebooks. He tells Tacitus to bring tablets next time he hunts.
Caveats: (1) Pliny was a Roman senator, consul in AD 100 and later a provincial governor. He is read today as a letter writer, but the safety rule says no politicians or government leaders of any time: the owner must decide. (2) Irony: he was sitting ("my beloved inactivity"). (3) A hunt. (4) Melmoth's English is free ("brisk bodily exercise" for "the stirring and movement of the body").

### 8. galen-small-ball (theme fit 8)
Galen, On Exercise with the Small Ball (De parvae pilae exercitio), ch. 3 (Kühn V 904; Marquardt 1879 pp. 5-6). 2nd century AD. Addressed to Epigenes.
English, F. A. Wright, Greek Athletics (London: Jonathan Cape, 1925), p. 116 (page image checked):
> Moreover, the wits are sharpened by the game: you have to think carefully how best to stop your opponent, and not drop the ball yourself. Thought by itself makes a man thin; but when it is combined with exercise and the pleasant rivalry of a sport it is of the very greatest benefit. The body improves in health, the mind is turned to practical knowledge. When exercise can render service both to body and mind, each in its own special form of excellence, it is a blessing indeed.

Greek (Marquardt 1879, via First1KGreek):
> τοῦτο δὲ καὶ τὴν γνώμην θήγει τῇ φροντίδι τοῦ τε μὴ καταβαλεῖν καὶ τοῦ διακωλῦσαι. φροντὶς δὲ μόνη μὲν καταλεπτύνει, μιχθεῖσα δέ τινι γυμνασίῳ καὶ φιλοτιμίᾳ καὶ εἰς ἡδονὴν τελευτήσασα τὰ μέγιστα καὶ τὸ σῶμα πρὸς ὑγίειαν καὶ τὴν ψυχὴν εἰς σύνεσιν ὀνίνησιν. οὐ σμικρὸν δὲ καὶ τοῦτʼ ἀγαθόν, ὅταν ἄμφω τὸ γυμνάσιον ὠφελεῖν δύνηται, καὶ σῶμα καὶ ψυχήν, εἰς τὴν ἰδίαν ἑκάτερον ἀρετήν.

Second line, opening of the treatise (Wright p. 113; Kühn V 899-900): "The best forms of gymnastic, I think, are those which are able not only to work the body but also to delight the mind." Greek: φημὶ γὰρ ἄριστα μὲν ἁπάντων γυμνασίων εἶναι τὰ μὴ μόνον τὸ σῶμα διαπονεῖν, ἀλλὰ καὶ τὴν ψυχὴν τέρπειν δυνάμενα.
Context: a short essay by the most famous doctor of the Roman world. He argues that catching games with a small ball are the best exercise: cheap, open to the poor, work the whole body, train the eye and sharpen the mind.
Slide cut: "When exercise can render service both to body and mind, each in its own special form of excellence, it is a blessing indeed."
Caveats: Wright translates freely ("the mind is turned to practical knowledge" for "it benefits the soul toward understanding"). The same essay says (p. 113) "the mind is more important than the body can be". Right after the quote Galen compares the game to a general's skills: leave out.

### 9. thoreau-legs-move-thoughts-flow (theme fit 6, "walking gives ideas" class)
Henry David Thoreau, Journal, 19 August 1851. The Writings of Henry David Thoreau, vol. VIII (Journal II), ed. Bradford Torrey, Houghton Mifflin 1906, pp. 404-405 (Gutenberg #59031).
> How vain it is to sit down to write when you have not stood up to live! Methinks that the moment my legs begin to move, my thoughts begin to flow, as if I had given vent to the stream at the lower end and consequently new fountains flowed into it at the upper. A thousand rills which have their rise in the sources of thought burst forth and fertilize my brain. You need to increase the draught below, as the owners of meadows on Concord River say of the Billerica Dam. Only while we are in action is the circulation perfect. The writing which consists with habitual sitting is mechanical, wooden, dull to read.

Context: a private journal entry on writing. He names the brain: movement in the legs makes the thoughts flow and "fertilize my brain".
Slide cut: "Methinks that the moment my legs begin to move, my thoughts begin to flow"
Caveats: one man's experience, not a claim about development. The paragraph just before it attacks church institutions: do not screenshot the page.

### 10. emerson-walking-gymnastics-for-the-mind (theme fit 7, "walking" class)
Ralph Waldo Emerson, "Country Life", lecture first read in March 1858 at Freeman Place Chapel, Boston. First printed 1904 in Natural History of Intellect and Other Papers (Complete Works, Centenary Edition, vol. XII), p. 141 (page image checked).
> Walking has the best value as gymnastics for the mind. "You shall never break down in a speech," said Sydney Smith, "on the day on which you have walked twelve miles."

Context: a lecture on life outdoors. Emerson goes on to the English universities, where "the reading men" row, ride or walk eight to ten miles daily.
Slide cut: "Walking has the best value as gymnastics for the mind."
Caveats: printed after his death from the lecture manuscript. On the next page he quotes "Plato" on exercise ("it would almost cure a guilty conscience"): that Plato line is unverified, do not reuse it (Dropped 3).

## Dropped / do not use

1. FAKE "Plato": "Lack of activity destroys the good condition of every human being, while movement and methodical physical exercise save it and preserve it." No dialogue or Stephanus number is ever given and the wording is in no translation I opened. The real text it grew from is Theaetetus 153b (above).
2. FAKE "Plato": "In order for man to succeed in life, God provided him with two means, education and physical activity. Not separately, one for the soul and the other for the body, but for the two together. With these two means, man can attain perfection." A loose rewrite of Republic III 411e-412a (real text in candidate 5). "Succeed in life" and "attain perfection" are not in Plato.
3. UNVERIFIED "Plato": exercise "would almost cure a guilty conscience". Only source found: Emerson's lecture "Country Life" (Works XII p. 142). Not found in Plato.
4. WRONG SENSE "Cicero": "It is exercise alone that supports the spirits, and keeps the mind in vigor." The words come from James Logan's translation of Cato Major (De Senectute) 11.36, printed by Benjamin Franklin, Philadelphia 1744: "The Body, we know, when overlaboured, becomes heavy, and, as it were, jaded; but 'tis Exercise alone that supports the Spirits, and keeps the Mind in Vigour." Latin: "et corpora quidem exercitationum defetigatione ingravescunt, animi autem exercitando levantur." Cicero means exercising the MIND, set against tiring the body. Also Cicero was a consul: out under the safety rule.
5. Cicero, De Senectute 36 (real text): says the mind needs "much more" care than the body. Against the theme, and a politician.
6. Seneca: Letter 15 argues the other way ("Stulta est enim ... occupatio exercendi lacertos": it is foolish for an educated man to work at building his arms; "Quidquid facies, cito redi a corpore ad animum": whatever you do, come back quickly from body to mind). De Tranquillitate Animi 17.8 has a real line on open-air walks (Aubrey Stewart, Bohn's Classical Library, 1889, 1900 printing: "It does good also to take walks out of doors, that our spirits may be raised and refreshed by the open air and fresh breeze") but it runs straight into advice to drink wine "even to intoxication". Seneca was also a consul and an emperor's adviser.
7. Quintilian, Institutio Oratoria I.11.15 (H. E. Butler, Loeb 1920): allows "a certain amount of time to the teacher of gymnastics" but only for posture and gesture, and warns against those who "kill the mind by over-attention to the body". Against the theme.
8. Russian-internet "Aristotle": «Ничто так не истощает и не разрушает человека, как продолжительное физическое бездействие». No work or Bekker number is ever given; one search found no primary source. Do not use for the Russian audience. Use the real Politics lines instead.
9. Marcus Aurelius: emperor, out by the safety rule (not researched).
10. Juvenal "mens sana" and Xenophon III.12.8: already used (brief).
