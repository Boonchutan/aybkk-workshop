## 2. Stakes and why care

Stakes: If you read a teacher's "no" as "he doesn't care", you leave the one teacher who would notice you are gone. Then you practise where nobody sees you. One missed week turns into a month, and next year you start again from day 1.

Why care: If a teacher ever said no to you and you felt pushed away, this story shows the other side. A "no" means somebody looked at you closely. That same person is the one who notices the morning your mat is empty.

## 3. Toolkit

Toolkit pick: /hook contrarian lever (bank #101) + Fork-First Story.

Why: The surprise of this story is the "no". A teacher says no four times, and then he is the one who goes looking. A contrarian hook flips "strict = doesn't care" in 4 seconds. Then the fork after the fourth no ("What you do?") makes the viewer choose before they hear that Upamanyu just says OK and goes back to the cows (Harvard #1).

Toolkit choice:
- Triple Hook #121 + /story 4-beat. Strongest quitting line, but it is the v4 hook and writer a's lane, and it keeps the "no" twist hidden until about 0:15.
- Vibe Samurai (flat statement, ladder of 3 no's, turn, flip). The no's make a natural ladder, but the turn line pulls toward a "not X but Y" line, and the spoken close gets longer.

## 4. Hook bank

#101 [bold] fit=adapt goal=shala habit=10
- template: "When will people realize {belief}?"
- aybkk: "When will people realize 'strict' often just means someone is paying attention?"
- bank note: "Own the 'strict' word kindly, and never sound defensive or argue with the review."

How I adapted it:
- I split it into a lean and a snap, two short lines in his voice: "You think strict teacher don't care." / "Strict often mean he's watching you."
- I dropped "When will people realize". It scolds the viewer and spends 2 seconds before the point.
- I kept "often" because it is his view, not a fact. Never "always".
- "Paying attention" became "watching you". It is a simpler word and talks to one person.
- The bank note is followed: the line defends nobody, names no review and no studio. It is about the teacher in the story.
- Then I added a story double hook (line 3: "say no four times. Then he come looking"). The point is out by about 0:04 and the loop is open by about 0:08.

Why not the others: #116 and #117 name the gap but give away the payoff. #106 is about asana progress, which is Lane A. #121 is not allowed for this writer.

## 5. Harvard tricks used

- #1 Decide before you know the ending: line 11 ("What you do?" plus a 1 s pause). The answer comes in line 12 ("He say OK.").
- #21 Teach them something about their own problem: lines 1 to 2 (a strict "no" means he is watching you) and line 22 ("To say no, he must watch you."). The story proves it at lines 15 to 16 (Who notice? The strict teacher).
- #27 Every claim needs a because: line 17 gives the teacher's own because, from the source ("That's why he's late."). Line 22 is the because for the hook's claim: you cannot say no to something you never saw. The source backs this up: every evening the teacher looks, sees Upamanyu is still well fed, and asks how he eats (Ganguli lines 1825, 1834, 1847, 1856).
- #30 Sell only the next step: caption only, with ONE ask ("Comment aybkk and I'll send you the link."). Nothing is sold out loud. No price, no course, no link line in the video.

Not used: #2 and #20 (rule: at least 3, never all).

## 8. Caption (English)

Hidden Stories of Ashtanga · Ep. 6 · The Teacher Who Said No

A teacher in old India said no to his student four times.

He took all the food the student begged.

No to begging again.

No to the cows' milk without asking.

No to the foam the baby cows spill when they drink.

Every time, the student said OK and went back to the cows.

One day he was so hungry he ate bad leaves. He could not see. He fell into a well.

At sunset, the strict teacher saw he was not home.

He went into the forest and called his name.

The story is in the Mahabharata, a very old, very long story book from India. Book 1, part 3.

The book calls it "the trial of Upamanyu". A trial is a test.

To say no to you, a teacher has to watch you.

At AYBKK, a teacher notices when you miss class.

Comment aybkk and I'll send you the link.

Why keep Samasthitih: In the book, the one thing Upamanyu does every single evening, even after four no's, is stand in front of his teacher and bow. The Sanskrit says it with the same "stand" word that is inside Samasthitih. In our room, Samasthitih is the first thing every morning, standing at the front of your mat where the teacher sees you. No other asana has a truer link to this story. "Sama" can mean "same", which also fits "same practice, same time".

## 10. Flags

1. Ganguli's sunset line has a hole: "is, of course, and therefore, doth not come home". The Sanskrit fills it (1.3.53C "sa niyataṃ kupitaḥ" = "he is surely angry"; Monier-Williams kupita: "provoked, incensed, offended, angry"). So the teacher thinks his "no" made the student angry, and he goes looking anyway. That is the strongest Habit Gap line in the story. Optional swap for line 17: "Maybe he angry with me. Let's go find him." It comes from the Sanskrit, not Ganguli's English, so use it only if Boonchu OKs using the Sanskrit. The script as written uses only Ganguli.
2. A critic could say the teacher's no's caused the hunger, the blindness and the fall. The script tells the facts and judges nobody. The teacher's help is real but limited: he notices, he searches, and he tells Upamanyu how to pray. Never say the teacher pulled him out. The book never says how he climbs out. The caption adds "the trial of Upamanyu" so it reads as a test, not cruelty. Do not draw any lesson about fasting or eating less.
3. Line 2 ("Strict often mean he's watching you") is Boonchu's opinion, from the bank #101 note. Keep "often". It is no study and no promise.
4. Line 25 ("Who come looking?") is a question only. It promises no calls, messages or visits from AYBKK. The only AYBKK fact used is the confirmed one, in the caption: "a teacher notices when you miss class." Many China and Russia viewers practise at home or online, so the caption invites and does not scold home practice.
5. Left out on purpose: the cake test (adi.txt 1958-1969). The Ashvins give Upamanyu a cake and he will not eat it before offering it to his teacher. They say his teacher once ate theirs without offering it, so "thy master's teeth are of black iron. Thine shall be of gold." It is a second lesson (one ordeal per video), and the iron-teeth line can read as mocking the teacher. It could be a later episode. The long Rig Veda-style prayer is left out too. The gods are named only on a small screen card, with respect.
6. "Pit" vs "well": the narrator says "pit" and Upamanyu himself says "well". We use his word.
7. Small retold words a purist might poke at:
   - "baby cows" = calves.
   - "bad leaves" = arka leaves with "pungent, acrimonious, crude, and saline properties". Arka is Calotropis, the crown flower, ดอกรัก in Thai. That is a nice detail for the Thai caption, but check the plant name before using it.
   - "Cow milk? No." The source's no is "without my consent", so the caption says "without asking".
   - "Strict" is our word. The source says "restrained" and "trial".
   - The teacher's name (Ayoda-Dhaumya) is left out to save a hard name.
   - Do not say he is Dhaumya the Pandavas' priest. Do not say this Upamanyu is the one in the Anushasana Parva. Neither is known (see v2 notes).
   - Upamanyu's age is not given: "student", never "boy".
8. Samasthitih repeats: it is this episode's own asana from v4 (stories-used: "Samasthitih ('this is our bow')"). It is also the first and last frame of Surya Namaskara A in the Aruna script (10 Oct) and the guru Reel option (12 Oct). Post them apart. "sama = same" is one of the Monier-Williams meanings. Teachers usually say "equal standing", so the screen card shows both "even" and "same".
9. Batch filming: this body leans on the "strict" hook (lines 1 to 3). After #121 instead, lines 15 to 16 ("Who notice? Strict teacher.") still work, but line 16's "strict" lands softer.
10. Content safety: no politics, no royalty, no death, no killing. Hunger and blindness are mild for bedtime, and v4 was accepted with them. Begging for alms is a student's practice in the story. Thai viewers know it from monks' alms rounds, and it is told with respect.
11. Timing: about 60 s. If it runs long, use the three trims in part 7. Do not cut lines 11 to 12 (the fork) or line 17 (the because).
