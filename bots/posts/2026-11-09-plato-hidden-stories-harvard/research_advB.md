## 2. Named asanas and why most are out

Already used (skip): Marichyasana (Marichi, Ep. on Marichyasana A), the bow (Dhanurasana: Drona's bird; Urdhva Dhanurasana: lamp),
the crane (Bakasana), the rooster and hen (Kukkutasana), the dove (Kapotasana). So Akarna Dhanurasana, Padangustha Dhanurasana,
Eka Pada Dhanurasana, Marichyasana E to H, Parsva Bakasana, Punga Kukkutasana and Eka Pada Kapotasana are out as repeats.

Out for safety:
- Nahusasana (Nahusha): every Nahusha story is about a throne. Adi Parva (Ganguli, Gutenberg #15474) says he "made the Rishis pay tribute
  and carry him on their backs like beasts of burden" and "ruled as if he were Indra himself". Vana Parva sections CLXXVIII to CLXXIX:
  he is now a huge snake that has caught Bhima "for food", and the questions are about "who is a Brahmana" (caste). Throne, a man about
  to be eaten, caste talk. Out.
  The other Nahusha story (Anushasana Parva sections L to LI, Gutenberg #15477: the sage Chyavana pulled out of the river in a fishing net)
  is out too: "the fish enmeshed in the net yielded up their lives", and the king offers "half thy kingdom". Deaths and a throne. Out.
- Vrschikasana (scorpion): no old scorpion story found (Jatakas vol. 1 to 6 have no scorpion tale; Mahabharata only lists scorpions).
  AYI links it to Shiva drinking the poison at the churning of the ocean. That is AYI's own parallel, the churning is already used
  (tortoise, Bhujapidasana), and the poison story is famous. The "saint and the scorpion who keeps stinging him" is modern lore,
  not in any old text I found. Out.
- Akarna Dhanurasana also has no fresh archer: Arjuna is used twice, Ekalavya ends with a cut thumb, Rama's bow stories are war or a royal wedding.
- No story: Mula Bandhasana, Tadasana, Samanasana, Paryangasana, Parivrttasana, the Dandasanas, Sama Konasana, Omkarasana
  (the Om stories I know are gods against demons, or Nachiketa and the god of death).

Left, with a real old story: Buddhasana, Shayanasana, Kapilasana.

## 4. The candidates (ranked)

### C1. Buddhasana: the teacher who saw his best student nodding off (best)

Name: buddha = "awakened, awake" (Monier-Williams p. 733). Sweeney: Buddhasana = "Enlightened posture". AYI: "The posture of the Buddha".
The asana: one leg goes behind the head and then behind the opposite shoulder, the arm binds around it (AYI, Buddhasana page).
It comes 7th in Advanced B, after Nahusasana, Vrschikasana and Shayanasana, so you are already tired when it comes.

Story (retold): Moggallana is one of the Buddha's two chief students. He sits down to meditate. His head starts to drop. He is nodding off.
The Buddha is far away, in another land. But he sees it. He disappears and appears right in front of Moggallana, as easily as you bend your arm.
He sits down and asks: "Are you nodding off, Moggallana?" "Yes, sir." No scolding. The Buddha gives him a ladder of small tries.
Change what you are thinking about. If that does not work, think over the teaching. If that does not work, say it out loud.
If that does not work, pull both your ears and rub your arms and legs. If that does not work, get up, wash your eyes with water,
look around, look up at the stars. If that does not work, think of light, day and night. If that does not work, walk slowly, back and forth.
And if nothing works? Lie down on your right side, one foot on the other, and keep in mind the time to get up.
When you wake, get up quickly.
(My count: 8 steps, the first one plus 7 "But what if that doesn't work?". Thai sources also say "8 ways".)

Source: Anguttara Nikaya 7.61, "Nodding Off" (Pacalayamana Sutta), tr. Bhikkhu Sujato, SuttaCentral (an7.61), Pali root on the same page.
Rank of Moggallana: Digha Nikaya 14 (dn14:1.9.7) and Anguttara Nikaya 1.190, SuttaCentral, Sujato.
Exact words checked (Sujato, segment numbers):
- 1.3: "Venerable Mahāmoggallāna was nodding off while meditating." (Pali "pacalāyamāno nisinno hoti")
- 1.4: "The Buddha saw him with his clairvoyance that is purified and superhuman."
- 1.6: "as easily as a strong person would extend or contract their arm, he vanished ... and reappeared in front of Mahāmoggallāna"
- 2.1: "Are you nodding off, Moggallāna? Are you nodding off?" 2.2: "Yes, sir."
- 3.1: "But what if that doesn't work? Then think about and consider the teaching as you've learned and memorized it"
- 5.1: "Then pinch your ears and rub your limbs." (Pali "ubho kaṇṇasotāni āviñcheyyāsi, pāṇinā gattāni anumajjeyyāsi", both ears)
- 6.1: "get up from your seat, flush your eyes with water, look around in every direction, and look up at the stars and constellations."
- 8.1: "walk mindfully, focusing on the perception of before and behind"
- 9.1: "lie down in the lion's posture—on the right side, placing one foot on top of the other—mindful and aware, and focused on the time of getting up."
- 9.2: "When you wake, you should get up quickly"
- dn14:1.9.7: "I have a fine pair of chief disciples named Sāriputta and Moggallāna." an1.190: "… with psychic power is Mahāmoggallāna."
Small real details for bedtime: the Buddha was staying at a place called "Crocodile's Bellow" (1.2). Each step ends with
"It's possible that you'll give up drowsiness in this way."

Surprise: the Buddha's great student fell asleep while meditating. The teacher saw it from far away and came. And his advice is so ordinary:
pull your ears, splash water on your eyes, look at the stars. The last step is not "try harder". It is "rest, then get up quickly".
Note: Thai Buddhists who read dharma may know it (it is on Thai education and newspaper sites as "8 ways to beat sleepiness").
Viewers in China, Russia and the West almost never know it. The ear-pulling and the stars are the surprise for everyone.

Practice lesson: Boonchu's own cue (28 Oct): "The hard moment in the practice is not always because you cannot do it, it is because you are tired."
By Buddhasana you are tired. Tired is not the signal to stop. It is the signal to change one small thing: the breath, the gaze, the way in.
AYI's own tip for this asana: after you hold the shin, lie down diagonally to the side on the forearm, slide the foot under the armpit, then sit up.

Habit Gap: people stop because they are tired, then feel bad, then stay away. Here the teacher noticed the moment it happened and gave
the next small step, one at a time. Even the rest step ends with "get up quickly". AYBKK fact you may say: a teacher notices when you miss class.

### C2. Shayanasana: the snake who holds up the world asked for one gift: to love the practice

Name: śayana = "lying down, resting, sleeping ... a bed, couch" (Monier-Williams p. 1055). Sweeney: "Couch (yogi reclining) posture".
Śeṣa = "a celebrated mythological thousand-headed serpent ... also called An-anta, 'the infinite' ... sometimes represented as forming the couch
and canopy of Viṣṇu whilst sleeping ... sometimes as supporting ... the entire world" (Monier-Williams p. 1088).
So the link is honest but it is a picture: "Shayana means couch or rest. The most famous couch in India is a snake." No old book says
the asana is named after Shesha. AYI says the same picture: Vishnu resting on Shesha.
Second link, for the series name: sthira = "firm ... fixed, immovable, motionless, still, calm ... steady" (Monier-Williams p. 1264).
Advanced B is "Sthira Bhaga". In this story Brahma's words are "susthirā" (very steady, MW p. 1239) and "acalā" (unmoving).
The asana: from forearm balance you come onto the tips of the elbows, hands hold the chin, body straight up, 5 breaths.
AYI: send the weight down through chin and hands too, and find the balance with fine work between belly and back.

Story (retold): Shesha is a snake. He has many brothers. They are jealous and quarrel all the time. Shesha wants a quiet life.
So he goes away and practises, alone, in the mountains and holy places, living on air, keeping his vows.
Brahma, the creator, sees him. Shesha is only skin and bone. Brahma asks him: "What are you doing, Shesha?" and "tell me the wish in your heart".
Brahma is pleased with him and offers a gift. Anything. Shesha does not ask for power. He asks: let my heart always enjoy goodness,
calm and practice. Brahma is very happy. Then he gives Shesha a job. "The earth wobbles. Hold her, so she is steady."
Shesha says yes. He goes down under the earth and holds the whole world on his head. Alone. And it stays steady.

Source: Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Astika Parva, Ganguli section XXXVI (Gutenberg #15474, vol. 1). Critical edition 1.32 (GRETIL Sanskrit, mbh01).
Exact words checked (Ganguli):
- "leaving his mother practised hard penances, living upon air and rigidly observing his vows"
- "Brahma, saw that ascetic with knotted hair, clad in rags, and his flesh, skin, and sinews dried up"
- "What is that thou doest, O Sesha? Let the welfare of the creatures of the worlds also engage thy thoughts." (Gutenberg prints "thorn doest", a typo)
- "Like enemies they are always jealous of one another." (about his brothers)
- "it is fortunate that thy heart hath been set on virtue. Let thy heart be more and more firmly set on virtue."
- "this is the boon desired by me; viz., may my heart always delight in virtue and in blessed ascetic penances"
- "Bear thou, O Sesha, properly and well this Earth so unsteady ... so that she may be steady."
- "I will, even as thou sayest hold the Earth steady. Therefore, O lord of all creatures, place her on my head."
- "lives underneath the Earth, alone supporting the world"
Sanskrit checked (CE 1.32):
- 1.32.5c "pariśuṣkamāṃsatvaksnāyuṃ" (flesh, skin and sinew dried up). 1.32.6c "kim idaṃ kuruṣe śeṣa" (what are you doing, Shesha?)
- 1.32.16c "dharme bhavatu susthirā" (may your mind be very steady in goodness)
- 1.32.17c "dharme me ramatāṃ buddhiḥ śame tapasi ceśvara" (may my mind DELIGHT in goodness, in calm and in practice, O Lord).
  ram = "to be glad or pleased, rejoice at, delight in, be fond of" (MW p. 867). So the wish really is "let me enjoy it".
- 1.32.19c "saṃgṛhya tiṣṭhasva yathācalā syāt" (hold her, so that she does not move). 1.32.20c "dhārayitāsmi niścalāṃ" (I will hold her motionless).

Surprise: almost everyone knows the picture of Vishnu lying on the snake. Almost nobody knows how the snake got the job. He was a brother
who left the quarrels to practise, and when the creator offered him anything, he did not ask for strength or a result. He asked to keep
enjoying the practice. That one wish is what made him the one who holds the world steady.

Practice lesson: in Shayanasana you cannot force the balance. It holds when the base is quiet and the small adjustments are calm.
Strength without that calm wobbles. The series is called "the steady part", and the steadiness comes from many calm mornings.
[BOONCHU'S OWN CUE: 5 words or less].

Habit Gap: the people who stay for years are not the ones who want results the most. They are the ones who learn to like the practice itself.
Shesha's wish is a habit wish. Harvard #2 (sell the hole): the result in life is "steady", in the body and in the day.

Hook ideas:
- #10 adapted: "The one who holds up the whole world asked for one gift. To enjoy the practice." (Contrarian: the viewer thinks results keep you going.)
- #7 adapted: "Want to keep practising for years? Wish for what this snake wished for."
- Watch the banned "not X but Y" pattern: do not write "He did not ask for power, he asked for..." as a slogan line. Tell it as story steps.
- Harvard #1 fork: "Creator says: ask me anything. What you ask?" Stop. Then tell.
- Harvard #20: "If you only practise for results, the first slow month ends it."
Lane B (comment aybkk).
Stakes idea: if you practise only for the result, you quit when the result is slow. Why care: everyone has a slow month.

Safety: kind story, no fight, no death. Leave out: the mother's curse (that the snakes will burn in a sacrifice), "wicked" brothers
(say "they quarrelled all the time"), and his wish to "cast off this body" (1.32.12: it sounds like wanting to die). Leave out Garuda.
Do not present "living on air" or "skin and bone" as advice. Say it as the old story only, or skip it.
The 10 Oct myths check found Brahma is pleased, not scolding: never tell it as "he practised too hard and got told off".
Never mock Brahma or Shesha. Shesha is also honoured in the Ashtanga opening chant as Patanjali ("sahasra sirasam svetam", thousand white heads):
that Patanjali IS Shesha is tradition, not this text. You may say "Ashtanga people chant to Patanjali, the old pictures show him with a snake's
thousand heads", and flag it as tradition. Family values (Thai, Chinese audience): do not make "leave your family" the lesson.
Say "he went to find a quiet place to practise".
Risk: the asana link is a picture (couch = snake bed), not a naming. Say it that way.

### C3. Kapilasana: the great teacher's first student was his own mother

Name: Kapila = "an ancient sage (identified by some with Viṣṇu and considered as the founder of the Sāṃkhya system of philosophy)"
(Monier-Williams p. 250). Sweeney: "Sage Kapila's posture". AYI: "The posture of Kapila".
The asana: Buddhasana plus a forward fold over the straight leg and one more bind (AYI: right hand around the right thigh to the left wrist).

Story (retold): Devahuti lives in a house in the forest, next to a lake, on the river Sarasvati. She is not young any more.
One day she says: "So much time has gone by." She feels she waited too long. She tells her son Kapila: "I am tired of chasing what my senses want.
I fell into the dark. You are my eye." Her son is a great teacher. He teaches her. Sit in a quiet clean place. Spread your seat.
Sit straight. Breathe in, hold, breathe out, so the mind becomes steady. Then Kapila asks his mother's permission and leaves, to the north-east.
Here is the fork. The teacher is gone. Does she stop? She does not move. She practises in that very same house, every day.
And "soon" she reaches the goal. The place gets a name: Siddhapada, "the place where she made it".

Source: Bhagavata Purana (vedabase.io, Prabhupada translation, Sanskrit on the same pages) 3.23.53, 3.25.7 to 3.25.8, 3.24.40, 3.28.8 to 3.28.9,
3.33.12 to 3.33.13, 3.33.30 to 3.33.31, 3.33.33.
Exact words checked:
- 3.23.53: "Until now we have simply wasted so much of our time ..." Sanskrit "etāvatālaṁ kālena vyatikrāntena" (enough time has gone by). Use the Sanskrit sense.
- 3.25.7: "I am very sick of the disturbance caused by my material senses ... I have fallen into the abyss of ignorance."
- 3.25.8: "You are my transcendental eye" (sac-cakṣuḥ)
- 3.24.40 (Kapila): "I shall also describe this sublime knowledge ... to My mother ... Thus she also will be freed from all material fear."
- 3.28.8: "spread a seat in a secluded and sanctified place, sit there in an easy posture, keeping the body erect, and practice breath control."
  Sanskrit "vijitāsana āsanam ... ṛju-kāyaḥ samabhyaset" (having mastered the asana ... body straight, practise).
- 3.28.9: "first he should inhale very deeply, then hold the breath in, and finally exhale ... so that the mind may become steady"
  Sanskrit "cittaṁ yathā sthiram acañcalam" (so the mind is sthira, steady, unshaking). Same word as Sthira Bhaga.
- 3.33.12: Kapila "took permission from her and left His home". 3.33.33: he "went towards the northeast".
- 3.33.13: "Devahūti also began to practice bhakti-yoga in that very āśrama." Sanskrit "tasminn āśrama" (in that same hermitage).
- 3.33.30: "Devahūti soon became liberated" Sanskrit "acirataḥ" (soon, before long).
- 3.33.31: "The place where Devahūti achieved her perfection ... is known all over the three worlds as Siddhapada."
Optional rehook inside the story (same text): the lake by her house was "called Bindu-sarovara because drops of tears had fallen there from the eyes
of the Lord, who was overwhelmed by extreme compassion for the sage" (3.21.38-39, Sanskrit "netrān nyapatann aśru-bindavaḥ"). The sage was her
husband Kardama. Use only if it does not break the one thread.

Surprise: the man the old books call the founder of Sankhya, the thinking behind yoga, had his own mother as his first student. She started late,
and said so. The teacher left, and she did not go anywhere. Same house, same seat, and she got there "soon".

Practice lesson: Kapilasana comes late in a late series, and many people reach it late in life. It is Buddhasana plus one more fold and one more bind.
You do not need a new place or a new method for the last piece. Same seat, same breath, one more small piece. [BOONCHU'S OWN CUE: 5 words or less].

Habit Gap: "I wasted too many years, it is too late" is one of the big reasons people never start or never come back. The story answers it:
start where you are, in the same place, with the same practice. It also fits the 180K pitch (build students who keep going), but keep the
AYBKK line separate: at AYBKK a teacher notices when you miss class.

Hook ideas:
- #83 adapted: "You think you started too late? This teacher's first student was his own mother."
- #72 adapted for the close: give it many messy mornings before you judge yourself (no "12 weeks" unless the script uses the real 12-week check).
- Harvard #1 fork: "The teacher leaves. The student is not young. What she do?" Stop. Then tell.
- Harvard #27 because: she got there "because she stayed in the same seat" (that is the text: same hermitage, then "soon").
Lane B (comment aybkk).
Stakes idea: if you believe you are too late, you never start, and next year you are one year later. Why care: most adults who come to the mat started late.

Safety: respectful. Kapila is honoured as a form of Vishnu in this book: never joke about a son teaching his mother. Do not say her married years
were "wasted": that reads as putting down family life (Thai and Chinese audiences). Say "so much time has gone by".
Leave out: the river made from her body at the end (3.33.32, it means her death), "her body became thin" and "old garments" (no austerity advice),
the rich flying palace, and Kapila's other famous story (the 60,000 sons burned by his look, Bhagavata 9.8): deaths.
Prabhupada's translation is devotional. Quote the Sanskrit sense where his English adds ideas (e.g. 3.33.14 "gray" hair: the Sanskrit "kapiśa" means
tawny or brownish, so do not say grey).
Risk: no fail-hero-payoff villain. The "fail" is her feeling of lost time. Weaker tension than C1 and C2.

### C4. Kapilasana (alternative): the lake made of a god's tears

Name and asana: as C3.
Story (retold): Kapila's father Kardama is told by Brahma to start a family. He goes to the river Sarasvati and practises there for ten thousand years.
Vishnu comes, pleased. He is so moved that tears fall from his eyes. The drops make a lake: Bindu-sarovara, "the lake of drops".
Vishnu promises: I will be born as your son, and I will teach. Then Vishnu leaves. Kardama does not go anywhere. He stays on the bank of the lake
and waits for the day. On that very day, a father arrives with his daughter Devahuti. Later their son is born: Kapila.
Source: Bhagavata Purana 3.21.6 to 3.21.8, 3.21.32, 3.21.33, 3.21.35, 3.21.37, 3.21.38-39 (vedabase.io).
Exact words checked:
- 3.21.6: "practiced penance on the bank of the river Sarasvatī for a period of ten thousand years" (sahasrāṇāṁ samā daśa)
- 3.21.8: "being pleased, showed Himself to that Kardama Muni"
- 3.21.32: "I shall manifest My own plenary portion through your wife, Devahūti ... and I shall instruct her"
- 3.21.35: "the worshipful sage Kardama stayed on the bank of Bindu-sarovara, awaiting the time of which the Lord had spoken." (taṁ kālaṁ pratipālayan)
- 3.21.37: "they reached the hermitage of the sage, who had just completed his vows of austerity on the very day foretold by the Lord."
- 3.21.38-39: "It was called Bindu-sarovara because drops of tears had fallen there from the eyes of the Lord, who was overwhelmed by extreme compassion"
Surprise: a god cries, and the tears become a lake. And after the promise, the sage just waits at the same lake.
Practice lesson: patience in the same place. Kapilasana takes years; you wait on "your bank".
Habit Gap: weak. Patience, yes; quitting or a teacher who notices, no.
Hook idea: #95 adapted ("The oldest idea in yoga: wait in the same place.") Lane B.
Safety: "ten thousand years" is the text's number, say "the old book says". Kardama asks for a family, that is fine at bedtime; skip any talk of the
wedding gifts and the flying palace. The father who arrives is Svayambhuva Manu, a king with a gold chariot (3.21.36): say only "a father came with his daughter".
Risk: the point (wait) is less surprising than the image (tears). Use it as the rehook inside C3 rather than its own Reel.

### C5. Buddhasana (alternative, REPEAT RISK): the slow student at the gate

Name and asana: as C1.
Story (retold): Little Panthaka (Culla-Panthaka, "Little Wayman" or "Little Roadling" in old English books) tries for four months to learn one short verse.
He cannot. His own big brother, a senior monk, tells him: "Go home now." Next morning, early, he walks out to leave. He stands sad at the gate.
But the Buddha got up even earlier and is walking up and down on his road. "Where are you going at this hour?" He touches his head, takes his arm,
brings him back in, and gives him one small thing to do: hold a clean white cloth, rub it, and repeat "removal of dirt". The cloth gets dirty
from his hand. He understands that everything changes, and he wakes up.
Sources: Theragatha 10.4 (his own verses), tr. Sujato, SuttaCentral thag10.4. Jataka No. 4, Cullaka-setthi-jataka, Cowell ed., vol. 1 (Chalmers tr., archive.org scan).

Surprise for most viewers: the teacher got up earlier than the student who was quitting, and waited on his road.
Practice lesson and Habit Gap: the strongest Habit Gap story here (about to quit, the teacher noticed first, one tiny daily practice). One small thing,
repeated, while the big thing (the verse, the full asana) will not come.
Hook idea: #117 adapted ("The part of teaching nobody sees: getting up earlier than the student who wants to quit."). Lane B.
Why it is last: (1) the white cloth was already PUBLISHED as a carousel slide on 5 Oct ("He could not learn one verse in four months. The Buddha gave him
a cloth to rub instead. It worked."), so the key moment is not new to our followers; (2) Thai Buddhists widely know Phra Culla-Panthaka and his cloth
chant; (3) "my brother told me go home" is very close to Ep. 6 "The Teacher Who Said No". Use only if Boonchu says a Reel retelling is OK.
Safety: respectful only. The cloth is "made by the Buddha's power" in the Jataka: skip that, say "a clean white cloth". Leave out the past-life part
(he once mocked a slow monk, which "caused" his slowness: that reads as blaming the slow), and the thousand copies of himself.
The Jataka's verses speak of "lust" as the real dirt: leave that out. No AI picture of the Buddha.
