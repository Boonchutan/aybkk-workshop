# Lane "educators": research notes (10 Nov 2026)

Theme: movement / physical exercise / the body comes first for the mind or brain. Pool: educators and child-development writers.

Used list (`used.txt`) checked. Not in it: Rousseau, Piaget, Froebel, Kephart, Gesell, Ayres. In it with a DIFFERENT line: Dewey ("An ounce of experience...", Democracy and Education ch. 11), Montessori ("forty-four repetitions"), Montaigne (the calf), Locke ("Nobody is made anything by hearing of rules"). None of the lines below repeats those.
Other lanes: "brainmove" already has Karen Adolph (Adolph and Hoch 2019), Thelen and Smith, Sechenov, Bernstein; "exercise" has Diamond 2000; "ancients" has Plato, Aristotle, Xenophon. Not repeated here.

How each line was checked
- FULL TEXT = I downloaded the whole work (Project Gutenberg, French Wikisource, archive.org) and read the passage.
- PAGE IMAGE = I also looked at the scan of the printed page.
- SNIPPET = the book is lending-only on archive.org; I rebuilt the sentence from overlapping search-inside snippets of the book itself (not from other books quoting it). Exact page is given only where a running head or the contents page shows it.

## The 10 candidates sent

### 1. rousseau-learn-to-think (fit 10) FULL TEXT, English + French
Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Émile, ou De l'éducation (1762), Book II. Translation: Barbara Foxley (Everyman's Library, J. M. Dent, 1911; the Gutenberg file names the translator, not the year).
- EN: "To learn to think we must therefore exercise our limbs, our senses, and our bodily organs, which are the tools of the intellect; and to get the best use out of these tools, the body which supplies us with them must be strong and healthy. Not only is it quite a mistake that true reason is developed apart from the body, but it is a good bodily constitution which makes the workings of the mind easy and correct."
- FR: « Pour apprendre à penser, il faut donc exercer nos membres, nos sens, nos organes, qui sont les instruments de notre intelligence ; et pour tirer tout le parti possible de ces instruments, il faut que le corps, qui les fournit, soit robuste et sain. Ainsi, loin que la véritable raison de l'homme se forme indépendamment du corps, c'est la bonne constitution du corps qui rend les opérations de l'esprit faciles et sûres. »
- Sentence just before: "our first teachers in natural philosophy are our feet, hands, and eyes." / « nos premiers maîtres de philosophie sont nos pieds, nos mains, nos yeux. »
- Second strong cut, same Book, a few pages earlier: "Would you cultivate your pupil's intelligence, cultivate the strength it is meant to control. Give his body constant exercise, make it strong and healthy, in order to make him good and wise; let him work, let him do things, let him run and shout, let him be always on the go; make a man of him in strength, and he will soon be a man in reason." / « Voulez-vous donc cultiver l'intelligence de votre élève ; cultivez les forces qu'elle doit gouverner. Exercez continuellement son corps ; rendez-le robuste et sain, pour le rendre sage et raisonnable ; qu'il travaille, qu'il agisse, qu'il coure, qu'il crie, qu'il soit toujours en mouvement ; qu'il soit homme par la vigueur, et bientôt il le sera par la raison. »
- Third: "It is a lamentable mistake to imagine that bodily activity hinders the working of the mind, as if these two kinds of activity ought not to advance hand in hand, and as if the one were not intended to act as guide to the other." / « C'est une erreur bien pitoyable d'imaginer que l'exercice du corps nuise aux opérations de l'esprit ; comme si ces deux actions ne devaient pas marcher de concert, et que l'une ne dût pas toujours diriger l'autre ! »
- URLs: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5427/pg5427.txt ; https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Émile,_ou_De_l'éducation/Édition_1852/Livre_II
- Wording check: the brief's version ("...the body which supplies us with these tools...") is slightly off. Foxley prints "with them".
- Care: right after the "cultivate" passage Rousseau compares "peasants and savages" (dated words): stop the cut before it.

### 2. rousseau-julie-body-before-mind (fit 10) FULL TEXT, French + 1912 English
Rousseau, Julie ou la Nouvelle Héloïse (1761), Part V, Letter III (Saint-Preux writes to Lord Edouard; the words are Julie's, explaining how she and her husband bring up their children). A novel in letters, so a character speaks, but it is the same teaching as Émile.
- FR: « La raison ne commence à se former qu'au bout de plusieurs années, et quand le corps a pris une certaine consistance. L'intention de la nature est donc que le corps se fortifie avant que l'esprit s'exerce. Les enfants sont toujours en mouvement ; le repos et la réflexion sont l'aversion de leur âge ; une vie appliquée et sédentaire les empêche de croître et de profiter ; leur esprit ni leur corps ne peuvent supporter la contrainte. »
- EN (R. L. Archer, ed., Rousseau on Education, Edward Arnold, London, 1912, "Extract from Julie", p. 28): "Reason begins to develop only after some years, when the body has reached a certain stage of development. Nature's intention is to strengthen the body before exercising the mind. Children are always in motion; quiet and meditation are their aversion; a studious or sedentary life is injurious to their health and growth; neither their minds nor their bodies can bear constraint."
- URLs: https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Julie_ou_la_Nouvelle_Héloïse/Cinquième_partie ; https://archive.org/details/rousseauoneducat00rousuoft
- Finding: John Dewey quotes exactly this English line in Democracy and Education (1916) and answers it (candidate 4). Quote sites give it to "Émile"; it is not in Émile Book II: it is in Julie.
- Care: the next sentence calls book-bound children "stupid rather than reasonable": cut before it.

### 3. montessori-mental-development-movement (fit 10) FULL TEXT + PAGE IMAGES
Maria Montessori, The Absorbent Mind, first edition, Theosophical Publishing House, Adyar, Madras, 1949 ("based upon the lectures given by Dr. Maria Montessori at Ahmedabad", introduction by Mario M. Montessori, Karachi, May 1949). Ch. XIII "Movement and Total Development".
- p. 203: "But mental development must be connected with movement and is dependent on it. This is the new idea that must enter educational theory and practice." ("must" is in italics in the book.)
- p. 203, next paragraph but one: "Our new conception stresses the importance of movement as a help to the development of the brain, once it is placed in relation to the centre. Mental development and even spiritual development can and must be helped by movement. Without movement there is no progress and no health (mentally speaking)."
- p. 204: "Watching him, one sees that he develops his mind by using his movements." and "...the child, scientifically observed, shows that he develops his intelligence generally through movement."
- p. 214 (ch. XIV "Intelligence and the Hand"): "The development of the hand therefore goes side by side with the development of the intelligence."
- URL: https://archive.org/details/absorbentmind031961mbp (p. 203 = scan leaf n218, p. 204 = n219, p. 214 = n229; images saved in dl_educators/pages/)
- TWO ENGLISH WORDINGS EXIST. The later standard edition is "Translated from the Italian by Claude A. Claremont" (translator's note dated September 1958). There ch. 13 is "The Importance of Movement in General Development" and the same lines read (SNIPPET, Dell 1978 and 1973 printings): "...mental development must be connected with movement and be dependent on it. It is vital that educational theory and practice should become informed by this idea." and "Watching a child makes it obvious that the development of his mind comes about through his movements." and (ch. 14) "The hands are the instruments of man's intelligence." Always say which edition a slide quotes.
- Also verified, another book (SNIPPET): The Secret of Childhood, translated by M. Joseph Costelloe, S.J., Fides Publishers, Notre Dame, 1966 (scan: Ballantine 1972), ch. 15 "Movement": "Movement, or physical activity, is thus an essential factor in intellectual growth, which depends upon the impressions received from outside. Through movement we come in contact with external reality, and it is through these contacts that we eventually acquire even abstract ideas." Italian original (Il segreto dell'infanzia, Garzanti) NOT opened, so kept as a spare, not a candidate.
- Older translation of the same book (FULL TEXT): The Secret of Childhood, tr. Barbara Barclay Carter, Longmans 1936 (Orient Longmans reprint), ch. "The Importance of Movement": "But movement is a part of man's very personality, and nothing can take its place." Do NOT use the sentence after it ("The man who does not move is injured in his very being and is an outcast from life") or the lines comparing deaf and blind people: hurtful today.

### 4. dewey-mind-by-exercise-of-muscles (fit 9) FULL TEXT
John Dewey, Democracy and Education (Macmillan, 1916), ch. 9 "Natural Development and Social Efficiency as Aims", section 1 "Nature as Supplying the Aim", point (2).
- "When he says that "Nature's intention is to strengthen the body before exercising the mind" he hardly states the fact fairly. But if he had said that nature's "intention" (to adopt his poetical form of speech) is to develop the mind especially by exercise of the muscles of the body he would have stated a positive fact. In other words, the aim of following nature means, in the concrete, regard for the actual part played by use of the bodily organs in explorations, in handling of materials, in plays and games."
- "he" = Rousseau (candidate 2).
- Also verified, ch. 11 "Experience and Thinking", section 1: "For the pupil has a body, and brings it to school along with his mind. And the body is, of necessity, a wellspring of energy; it has to do something." And: "Before the child goes to school, he learns with his hand, eye, and ear, because they are organs of the process of doing something from which meaning results." And: "It may be seriously asserted that a chief cause for the remarkable achievements of Greek education was that it was never misled by false notions into an attempted separation of mind and body."
- URL: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/852/pg852.txt
- Care: the key sentence is conditional ("if he had said"). Dewey means purposeful doing (exploring, handling, play, games); in ch. 11 he attacks mechanical muscle drill.

### 5. gesell-motor-basis-of-mental-life (fit 9) SNIPPET
Arnold Gesell (1880-1961), Infant Development: The Embryology of Early Human Behavior, Harper, New York, 1952, ch. 5 "The Total Action System" (subtitle "The Maturation of the Mind", starts p. 57; index: "Motor behavior, 58, 59"; exact page not visible).
- "It is probable that all mental life has a motor basis and a motor origin. The non-mystical mind must always take hold. Even in the rarefied realms of conceptual reasoning we speak of intellectual grasp and of symbolic apprehension."
- Sentence before: "...the infant's visual and manual behavior takes on voluntary and adaptive characteristics." After: "Thinking might be defined as a comprehension and manipulation of meanings." Same chapter: "...thinking has part of its genesis in the sensorimotor activities of eyes and hands." and "Language likewise has a motor basis."
- URL: https://archive.org/details/infantdevelopmen00gese
- Care: "It is probable" is his hedge: keep it. Gesell's theory is maturation: growth of the nervous system sets the pace, practice adds little. He supports "mind starts in movement", not "more exercise, more brain".

### 6. kephart-roots-in-motor-learning (fit 9) SNIPPET
Newell C. Kephart (1911-1973, Purdue University on the title page), The Slow Learner in the Classroom, C. E. Merrill Books, Columbus, Ohio, 1960, ch. 3 "Motor Bases of Achievement", section "The Muscular Basis of Behavior", p. 35 (the passage runs over to p. 36).
- "To a large extent, so-called higher forms of behavior develop out of and have their roots in motor learning. It is logical to assume that all behavior is basically motor, that the prerequisites of any kind of behavior are muscular and motor responses."
- Sentence before (1971 2nd edition wording): "In early childhood, mental and physical activities are closely related (Jersild, 1960), and motor activities play a major role in intellectual development."
- Sentence after: "Behavior develops out of muscular activity, and so-called higher forms of behavior are dependent upon lower forms of behavior, thus making even these higher activities dependent upon the basic structure of the muscular activity upon which they are built."
- URL: https://archive.org/details/slowlearnerincla00keph (same words in a second 1960 scan and in the 1971 edition)
- Care: the book title uses a label not used today. His perceptual-motor training programme was later tested and did not reliably raise school results: present the line as his view of how learning starts, not as a proven method.

### 7. piaget-intelligence-proceeds-from-action (fit 8) SNIPPET (English) + FULL TEXT (French OCR)
Jean Piaget and Bärbel Inhelder, La psychologie de l'enfant, Presses Universitaires de France, 1966, opening of ch. II. English: The Psychology of the Child, "Translated from the French by Helen Weaver", Basic Books, New York, 1969, ch. 2 "The Development of Perception", p. 28.
- EN: "As regards the development of the cognitive functions in the child, Chapter 1 has suggested, and the following chapters will confirm, that the sensori-motor structures constitute the source of the later operations of thought. This means that intelligence proceeds from action as a whole, in that it transforms objects and reality, and that knowledge, whose formation can be traced in the child, is essentially an active and operatory assimilation."
- FR: « En ce qui concerne le développement des fonctions cognitives chez l'enfant, le chapitre Ier nous a fait entrevoir, et les suivants le confirmeront, que les structures sensori-motrices constituent la source des opérations ultérieures de la pensée. Cela signifie donc que l'intelligence procède de l'action en son ensemble, en tant que transformant les objets et le réel, et que la connaissance, dont on peut suivre la formation chez l'enfant, est essentiellement assimilation active et opératoire. » (accents restored by me from a rough OCR)
- URLs: https://archive.org/details/psychologyofchil00piag ; French text read at https://archive.org/details/FRENCHPDF.COMLaPsychologieDeLenfantJeanPiaget
- Also verified (SNIPPET), Piaget alone: Six Psychological Studies, tr. Anita Tenzer, ed. David Elkind, Random House, 1967, ch. 1 "The Mental Development of the Child", pp. 8-9: "The period that extends from birth to the acquisition of language is marked by an extraordinary development of the mind. [...] In fact, it is no less than a conquest by perception and movement of the entire practical universe that surrounds the small child." French (Six études de psychologie, Gonthier, 1964): « La période qui s'étend entre la naissance et l'acquisition du langage est marquée par un développement mental extraordinaire. [...] il ne consiste pas moins, en effet, qu'en une conquête, par les perceptions et les mouvements, de tout l'univers pratique entourant le petit enfant. »
- Care: Piaget's "action" is the baby acting on things (grasping, looking, moving), not fitness training.

### 8. ayres-sensory-motor-foundation (fit 8) SNIPPET
A. Jean Ayres, Sensory Integration and the Child, Western Psychological Services, Los Angeles, 1979, ch. 1 "What Is Sensory Integration?", section "A Sensory Processing Machine" (about pp. 7-8; page not visible).
- "As the child grows older, mental and social responses replace some of this sensory-motor activity. However, the brain's mental and social functions are based upon a foundation of sensory-motor processes. The sensory integration that occurs in moving, talking, and playing is the groundwork for the more complex sensory integration that is necessary for reading, writing, and good behavior."
- Before it: "Until about the age of seven, the brain is primarily a sensory process[ing machine]..." and "...the first seven years of life are called the years of sensory-motor development."
- Short line elsewhere in the book: "Sensory-motor interaction provides the groundwork for later cognitive functions."
- URL: https://archive.org/details/sensoryintegrati0000ayre
- Care: about children to about age seven; "sensory-motor" means sensing plus moving. The therapy built on her theory has mixed trial evidence: quote it as her account of development, never as a treatment promise.

### 9. montaigne-not-a-soul-not-a-body (fit 7) FULL TEXT, English + French
Michel de Montaigne, Essais (1580), Book I, "De l'institution des enfans" (ch. 25 in Cotton, ch. 26 in modern editions). Translation: Charles Cotton (1685-86), revised by William Carew Hazlitt (1877).
- EN: "I would have his outward fashion and mien, and the disposition of his limbs, formed at the same time with his mind. 'Tis not a soul, 'tis not a body that we are training up, but a man, and we ought not to divide him. And, as Plato says, we are not to fashion one without the other, but make them draw together like two horses harnessed to a coach. By which saying of his, does he not seem to allow more time for, and to take more care of exercises for the body, and to hold that the mind, in a good proportion, does her business at the same time too?"
- FR: « Je veux que la bienseance exterieure, et l'entre-gent, et la disposition de la personne, se façonne quant et quant l'ame. Ce n'est pas une ame, ce n'est pas un corps qu'on dresse : c'est un homme ; il n'en faut pas faire à deux. Et, comme dict Platon, il ne faut pas les dresser l'un sans l'autre, mais les conduire également, comme une couple de chevaux attelez à mesme timon. Et, à l'ouir, semble il pas prester plus de temps et plus de sollicitude aux exercices du corps, et estimer que l'esprit s'en exerce quant et quant, et non au rebours. »
- The last French clause is stronger than Cotton: literally "and to judge that the mind gets its exercise along with them, and not the other way round."
- Sentence before: "...our very exercises and recreations, running, wrestling, music, dancing, hunting, riding, and fencing, will prove to be a good part of our study."
- Also verified, same essay: "It is not enough to fortify his soul; you are also to make his sinews strong; for the soul will be oppressed if not assisted by the members, and would have too hard a task to discharge two offices alone." / « Ce n'est pas assez de luy roidir l'ame ; il luy faut aussi roidir les muscles. Elle est trop pressée, si elle n'est secondée, et a trop à faire de seule fournir à deux offices. »
- URLs: https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3600/pg3600.txt ; https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Essais/Livre_I/Chapitre_26
- Care: he says "together", not "first". Around the "sinews" line he talks of weapons and beatings: do not extend that cut.

### 10. froebel-bodily-activity-strengthens-mind (fit 7) FULL TEXT English + PAGE IMAGE German
Friedrich Fröbel, Die Menschenerziehung (1826), § 90 ("Achtung, Kenntniß und Ausbildung des Körpers"). Translation: W. N. Hailmann, The Education of Man, D. Appleton, New York, 1887, p. 250.
- EN: "Furthermore, after severe mental activity, the body as well as the mind calls for strictly regulated, vigorous bodily activity, and this again reacts on the mind and strengthens it. Only where mental and bodily activity are thus in regular, living, mutual action and reaction, true life is possible."
- DE (W. Lange's edition, Enslin, 1863, p. 202, read on the page scan): „Ueberdieß fordert auch der Körper, oder, was man eben so gut sagen kann, der Geist nach einer angestrengten Geistesthätigkeit einer strenggeordneten, anstrengenden Körperthätigkeit, und diese so geordnete strenge Körperthätigkeit wirkt wieder stärkend auf den Geist zurück. Nur also, wo Körper- und Geistesthätigkeit in geordneter lebendiger Wechselverbindung stehen, ist wahrhaftes Leben."
- Same section, p. 249-250 / German p. 201: "Without such cultivation of the body, education can never attain its object, which is perfect human culture. Therefore, the body, like the mind, should in this respect pass through a true school..." / „Darum ohne eine solche Bildung des Körpers keine zur Vollkommenheit und zur vollendeten Ausbildung des Menschen führende Erziehung. Deshalb muß auch der Körper in dieser Hinsicht wie der Geist eine wahre Schule, doch nicht allein stehend, durchlaufen..."
- A second translation (Josephine Jarvis, A. Lovell, New York, 1885, p. 170) reads: "...this strict bodily activity thus methodized exerts again a strengthening reflex influence on the mind. There is true life, therefore, only where bodily and spiritual activity stand in methodized, active, reciprocal connection."
- URLs: https://archive.org/details/educationofman00fruoft ; https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_SMoJAQAAIAAJ (leaf 223 = p. 202) ; https://archive.org/details/educationofman00froe
- Care: he says body and mind work on each other, with bodily work after mental work; not "body first". The same section allows "punishment and severity": do not extend.

## Verified, not sent (spares)
- Locke, Some Thoughts Concerning Education (1693), §§ 1-2 (R. H. Quick's edition, Cambridge 1880, https://archive.org/details/somethoughtsconc00lock): "He, whose Mind directs not wisely, will never take the right Way; and he, whose Body is crazy and feeble, will never be able to advance in it." and "...though this be the principal Part, and our main Care should be about the Inside, yet the Clay-Cottage is not to be neglected. I shall therefore begin with the Case, and consider first the Health of the Body". Fit 5: Locke calls the mind "the principal Part"; he only starts with the body. His opening "A Sound Mind in a sound Body" is Juvenal's phrase (used before, OUT).
- Pestalozzi, Letters on Early Education Addressed to J. P. Greaves (London, 1827), Letter XXII (https://archive.org/details/lettersonearlyed00pestiala): "The revival of gymnastics is, in my opinion, the most important step that has been done in that direction." and "If the physical advantage of gymnastics is great and uncontrovertible, I would contend, that the moral advantage resulting from them is as valuable." Fit 5: he speaks of character and health, not of the mind.
- Pestalozzi, How Gertrude Teaches Her Children (1801), Letter XII, tr. Lucy E. Holland and Francis C. Turner, 1894, pp. 177-178 (https://archive.org/details/howgertrudeteach00pestuoft): "Striking and carrying, thrusting and throwing, drawing and turning, encircling and swinging, etc., are extremely simple expressions of our physical powers. [...] they contain [...] the foundations of all possible actions, even the most complicated, on which human callings depend." Fit 5: about practical skill.

## Dropped
- Sukhomlinsky, «Истоки способностей и дарований детей — на кончиках их пальцев. От пальцев, образно говоря, идут тончайшие ручейки, которые питают источник творческой мысли.» Always credited to «Сердце отдаю детям» (1969). I could not open the book (the one PDF found returned 403/404; no scan on archive.org). Seen only quoted in later books (e.g. П. Н. Андрианов, 1990). Secondary only. The short form «Ум ребёнка находится на кончиках его пальцев» looks like a paraphrase: found only in magazines.
- Karen Adolph: already held by lane "brainmove" (Adolph and Hoch 2019). Not repeated.
- Gesell, "Mind manifests itself": found only as a phrase his colleagues say he liked (Ames and others, 1970s books). Secondary. "The child thinks with his muscles" is not Gesell: it turns up in a 1919 book (Burger, Boy Behavior: "A boy thinks with his muscles to some extent at least") and in a 1967 film catalogue.
- "Nature's intention is to strengthen the body before exercising the mind" as a line of Émile: wrong book. It is in Julie (candidate 2).
- Pestalozzi "head, heart and hand" as a sentence: it is a later summary of his method; not found as a sentence in the two works opened.
- Piaget, "sensori-motor intelligence lies at the source of thought, and continues to affect it throughout life" (The Psychology of Intelligence, 1950): seen only in other books quoting it; one of them misquotes it as "Sensori-motor training lies at the source of thought". Not opened in Piaget's book: dropped.
- Piaget, "To know an object is to act on it" (Development and Learning, 1964): real and widely quoted, but about knowing by operating on things, not about movement or exercise; I saw it only in later reports. Dropped.
- Fröbel 1826 first edition at Deutsches Textarchiv and Piaget texts at the University of Geneva archive: both sites answered with a robot check; not read. The German Fröbel was read in the 1863 edition instead.

## Safety notes for the lane
- No politician, royalty, war or religious dispute in any chosen line. Montaigne served a term as mayor of Bordeaux; he is known as an essayist and was used before.
- No medical promise in any line. Kephart and Ayres wrote about children with learning difficulties: quote them on how learning starts, never as a cure.
- Rousseau: critics point out he left his own children at a foundling home; Archer's 1912 edition mentions it too.
- Montessori: the 1949 chapter also talks about heaven and reincarnation (pp. 200-201) and uses a "Minister of Foreign Affairs" image (p. 199): keep to pp. 203-204.
