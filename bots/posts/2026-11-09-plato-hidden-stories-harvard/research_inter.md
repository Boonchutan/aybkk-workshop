## CANDIDATE A: The Peacock Who Forgot Once (Pincha Mayurasana, or Mayurasana)

Name: piccha/piñcha = tail feather, especially a peacock's (MW p. 624). mayūra = peacock (MW p. 789). Pali "mora" = peacock.
Chinese 孔雀 = peacock.

Two old tellings of one story. Do not blend the two endings.

1) Chinese: 佛母大孔雀明王經 (Great Peacock Wisdom-King Sutra), translated by Amoghavajra (不空), Taisho vol. 19 no. 982,
p. 418a-b, with the "it was me" line at p. 419a. Text from the CBETA XML (github cbeta-org/xml-p5, T19n0982.xml).
Exact words checked:
- 418a: 「往昔之時，雪山南面，有金曜孔雀王於彼而住，每於晨朝，常讀誦佛母大孔雀明王陀羅尼，晝必安隱，暮時讀誦，夜必安隱。」
  (My plain translation: Long ago, on the south side of the Snow Mountains, lived a golden-bright peacock king. Every morning
  he said the Great Peacock words, and his day was safe. At dusk he said them, and his night was safe.)
- 418a-b: 「彼金曜孔雀王，忽於一時，忘誦此佛母大孔雀明王陀羅尼，遂與眾多孔雀婇女，從林至林，從山至山，而為遊戲，貪欲愛著，放逸昏迷，入山穴中。捕獵怨家，伺求其便，遂以鳥羂，縛孔雀王。被縛之時，憶本正念，即誦如前佛母大孔雀明王陀羅尼，於所緊縛，自然解脫，眷屬安隱，至本住處。」
  (One time he suddenly forgot to say them. He went off with many peahens, forest to forest, mountain to mountain,
  playing, carried away, careless, into a mountain cave. Hunters waiting for their chance tied him with a bird snare.
  Once tied, he remembered his right mind and said the words as before. The ropes came loose by themselves.
  He and his family were safe and went home.)
- 419a: 「往昔金曜孔雀王者，豈異人乎？即我身是。」 (The golden peacock king of long ago, was he someone else? He was me.)

2) Pali: Mora Jataka No. 159, Cowell vol. II (tr. W.H.D. Rouse, 1895), pp. 23-26 (Fausbøll pages [33]-[38]). Exact words checked:
- "When day dawned, as he sat upon the hill, watching the sun rise, he composed a Brahma spell to preserve himself safe in his own feeding-ground" [33]
- "he came back at even and sat on the hilltop to see the sun go down; then as he meditated, he uttered another spell" [35]
- "The hunter set snares in the peacock's feeding-ground. But even when the peacock stepped upon it, the snare would not close. This the hunter tried for seven years, but catch him he could not" [36]
- "The hunter observed that when the Golden Peacock came into the snare, it did not shut to, and also that he recited a charm before setting out in search of food." [37]
- "he set the snare ... early in the morning, before the peacock had recited his charm. Then he made the peahen utter a cry. This unwonted sound ... leaving his charm unsaid, he came towards her; and was caught in the net." [37]
- Ending: he talks with the king, then "rose up in the air, and flew back to the golden hill of Dandaka, with a parting word of advice: 'O king, be careful!'" [38]
- Frame: told "about a backsliding Brother" (a monk who was slipping away); at the end "the backsliding Brother became a Saint".

Story (Fail, Hero, Payoff; best told from the Chinese sutra because its ending has no king and no deaths):
A golden peacock. Every sunrise he says his morning words. Every sunset he says them again. One day he forgets.
He goes off playing with the peahens, forest to forest, hill to hill, into a cave. A snare ties him tight. (Fail.)
Fork: tied up, no strength helps. What does he do? He remembers. He says the same morning words again. (Hero.)
The ropes open by themselves, and he flies home. (Payoff.)
Optional rehook, from the Pali telling, credited in the caption: the hunter did not need a stronger net. He only needed
one sweet call before the morning words.

Surprise: he was not caught on a hard day. He was caught on a fun day. And what set him free was not strength.
It was coming back to the same small words.

Lesson for practice: Pincha Mayurasana goes wrong when you rush to kick the legs up and skip the base
(forearms, shoulders, gaze). The base is your "morning words". Same in the whole practice: the opening chant and
Surya Namaskara every morning. (The Pali peacock literally greets the rising sun.) Slot: [BOONCHU'S OWN CUE: 5 words or less].

Habit Gap: very strong. Daily habit, one skip on a sweet morning (phone, late night, holiday), trapped,
comes back, free. Close with a question like: "Last time you skipped, was it a hard day or a sweet one?"
Lane B (aybkk). Hook bank #86, flipped to contrarian ("You don't skip practice on the bad days. You skip on the sweet ones.");
other choice #83. Harvard: #1 fork (tied in the snare), #21 (you skip on sweet days), #20 (stay tied), #30.

Safety: the Chinese telling has no deaths, no kings. The Pali telling has hunters dying, a queen dying of longing,
six kings, and a king who wants to eat the bird to live forever. Leave all of that out. "Desire" becomes "a sweet new call".
It is the Buddha's own past life (both texts say so). Tell it with respect: "The Buddha told this story about his own past life."
Never say the words or the practice keep you safe from harm. That is the story's magic, not a promise.

Risks: Thai viewers know the chant. Thai news calls the Mora verse the "golden peacock" verse and says it is chanted
morning and evening (Thairath / thansettakij search results, 9 Nov). Most do not know the snare and the comeback, so the
twist should still land. Chinese viewers may know 孔雀明王 as a Buddhist protector. Keep the tone respectful. The two
tellings end differently, so name which one is told.

## CANDIDATE B: The Rock That Answered (Nakrasana)

Name: nakra = crocodile (MW p. 524). Nakrasana = the crocodile.

Source: Vanarinda Jataka No. 57, Cowell vol. I (tr. Robert Chalmers, 1895), pp. 142-143 (Fausbøll pages [278]-[280]).
Exact words checked:
- "the Bodhisatta used to leap from the bank on to this rock and thence on to the island. Here he would eat his fill of the fruits that grew on the island, returning at evening by the way he came. And such was his life from day to day."
- "For the story goes that the Bodhisatta always marked the exact height of the water in the river, and of the rock in the water. So, when he saw that, though the water stood at the same level, the rock seemed to stand higher out of the water, he suspected that a crocodile might be lurking there to catch him."
- "he shouted, as though addressing the rock, 'Hi! rock!' ... he shouted three times ... 'How comes it, friend rock, that you won't answer me to-day?'"
- "'Oh!' thought the crocodile; 'so the rock's in the habit of answering the monkey. I must answer for the rock to-day.' Accordingly, he shouted, 'Yes, monkey; what is it?'"
- "Open your mouth and catch me when I jump." ... "when crocodiles open their mouths, their eyes shut" (translator's footnote: "This assertion is not in accord with the facts of natural history.")
- "the wily monkey made a jump on to the crocodile's head, and thence, with a spring like lightning, gained the bank."
- Verse: "Whoso, O monkey-king, like you, combines / Truth, foresight, fixed resolve, and fearlessness, / Shall see his routed foemen turn and flee."

Story: A big strong monkey lives on a river bank. In the river is an island with mangoes, and halfway there one rock.
Every morning: bank, rock, island. Every evening: the same way home. Day after day. A crocodile wants to catch him
and lies on the rock. That evening the monkey looks. The water is the same height, but the rock is higher. (Fork:
jump or not?) He calls, "Hello, rock!" No answer. "Rock, why don't you answer me today?" (Rehook:) the crocodile thinks
the rock always talks, so he answers, "Yes, monkey?" Caught out. No other way home. (Worst moment.) The monkey says,
"Open your mouth, I'll jump in." The crocodile opens wide, his eyes close, and the monkey jumps on his head and onto the bank.
The crocodile praises him.
(Fail = the trap on his daily path; Hero = he noticed and tested; Payoff = home safe, and the crocodile says he was wise.)

Surprise: the monkey knew because he checked the water and the rock every single day. The same boring road is what
saved him. And a crocodile that answers for a rock is funny and new. The well-known monkey-and-crocodile tale
(heart in the fig tree, Jataka 208 and Panchatantra Book 4) is a different one.

Lesson for practice: in Nakrasana you are the crocodile on the rock: flat, hard, still, hips level with the shoulders,
then hop. If you do the same asana every day, you feel when the hips ride higher than usual, and so does the teacher.
Slot: [BOONCHU'S OWN CUE: 5 words or less].

Habit Gap: strong. "Same practice every day is not boring. It is how you notice." CONFIRMED FACT (OK to say): at AYBKK
a teacher notices when you miss class. Close: "If you change your practice every day, how will you know what changed?"
Lane B (aybkk). Hook bank #101 ("'strict' often just means someone is paying attention") or #10 flipped
("Same practice every morning? That's how the monkey stayed alive"). Harvard: #1 fork (rock too high), #21, #27 (he knew BECAUSE he measured daily), #30.

Safety: no one dies. The crocodile wanted "to catch you and eat your heart"; say "to catch you". Leave out the frame story
(Devadatta trying to kill the Buddha, and Cinca as the crocodile's wife): religious conflict. The monkey is the Bodhisatta.
Tell it kindly, with no joke about him. Fine for a 5-year-old (a classic children's tale).

Risks: "crocodiles shut their eyes when they open their mouths" is false (the translator says so). Say "his eyes closed"
inside the story. Never teach it as a fact. The crocodile is the bad one while the viewer "is" the crocodile in the asana,
so frame the asana link on how still and flat he lay, not on him being good.

## CANDIDATE C: The Thin Horses (Vatayanasana)

Name: vātāyana m. "'moving or fleet as wind', a horse" (MW p. 935). Ganguli's words in this very scene are "fleet as the
winds" and "steeds gifted with the speed of the wind": the same picture as the asana's name.

Source: Mahabharata, Vana Parva (Nalopakhyana), Ganguli tr., sections LXVII, LXXI, LXXII (Gutenberg ebook 15474).
Exact words checked:
- LXVII: "My name is Vahuka. There is no one in this world equal to me in managing steeds."
- LXXI: "Vahuka after much scrutiny and careful deliberation, selected some steeds that were lean-fleshed, yet strong and capable of a long journey ... born in (the country of) Sindhu, and fleet as the winds."
- LXXI: "the king said somewhat angrily, 'What is this, that thou wishest to do? Thou shouldst not jest with us. How can these horses of mine, weak in strength and breath, carry us?'"
- LXXI: "Each of these horses bears one curl on his forehead, two on his temples, four on his sides, four on his chest, and one on his back."
- LXXI: "the king without loss of time mounted upon the car, when those best of horses fell down upon the ground on their knees. Then ... Nala began to soothe horses endued with energy and strength. And raising them up with the reins ... And those best of steeds, duly urged by Vahuka, rose to the sky"
- LXXII: "Like a bird coursing through the sky, Nala soon crossed rivers and mountains"; the king's upper cloth falls and Nala says "The sheet is dropped down far away. We have travelled one yojana thence."
- The trip: "I will go to the city of the Vidarbhas in a single day."

Story: A man must reach a far city in one day. His new horse man, Vahuka, says nobody handles horses like him.
He walks the stable and picks thin horses. The man gets angry: "Don't joke with me. These are weak in strength and breath."
Vahuka shows him the small marks on each horse. (Fork: thin horses, long road, one day. You pick?) He agrees.
He climbs in, and the horses fall down on their knees. (Rehook and worst moment: now what? Whip them?)
Vahuka does not whip. He calms them and lifts them up with the reins. They rise and run like the wind, like a bird
in the sky. The man's shawl falls off, and by the time he asks to stop, it is already far behind.
(Fail = knees on the ground; Hero = calm hands and a trained eye; Payoff = fast as wind.)

Surprise: the "weak" thin horses were the right ones, they collapsed at the very first load, and the master's answer
was to calm them, not to push.

Lesson for practice: Vatayanasana takes one knee down to the floor and then you must rise again. On the first tries
you sink or drop onto the knee and cannot come up. Do not force it; calm the breath, lift from the standing leg, rise.
"Weak in strength and breath" are the king's own words: the breath comes first. Slot: [BOONCHU'S OWN CUE: 5 words or less].

Habit Gap: medium-strong. You think you are too thin, too stiff, too weak. A teacher who knows the marks sees what you cannot,
and the first fall is part of the trip, not the end. Close: "Who decided you are too weak? You, or someone who knows the marks?"
Lane B (aybkk), or Lane A (STUCK) if the Reel is about coming up from Vatayanasana. Hook bank #42 or #102, flipped
("The best horse in the old story was the thin one."). Harvard: #1 fork, #27 (the marks are the because), #21, #30.

Safety: Vahuka is King Nala in hiding. He lost his kingdom at dice and his looks were changed by a snake (earlier sections).
Leave all of that out, and leave out why they rush (Damayanti's announced second choosing). The rider, King Rituparna,
is a side figure only: say "a man" or "his master", with no throne and no crown. No war, no deaths. Do not mock looks.
The text says Vahuka is "of unsightly appearance", so say only "he did not look special".

Risks: Indian viewers may know Nala and Damayanti as a love story, but this horse scene is rarely told. The optional
twist "he was really the great Nala" pulls the king story back in, so skip it. Do not turn "one yojana" into km
(not checked).

## CANDIDATE D: The Foal Who Ran on Lotus Leaves (Vatayanasana, second choice)

Name: vātāyana = horse, "fleet as wind" (MW p. 935).

Source: Kundaka-kucchi-sindhava Jataka No. 254, Cowell vol. II (tr. Rouse, 1895), pp. 199-203 (Fausbøll pages [286]-[291]).
Exact words checked:
- "The woman loved the foal like a son; and she fed him upon parched rice drippings, on broken meats, and grass."
- "the horses scented this highbred foal ... and not one of them would enter the place."
- "the Bodhisatta noted his marks, and thought he, 'This is a priceless thoroughbred'"
- "the Bodhisatta thought he would make trial of the foal, whether he knew his own power or no."
- "Not a man could see him at all! Then he fastened a red leaf upon the horse's flank; and they saw just the leaf. And then he rode him over the surface of a pond ... not even the tips of his hoofs were wet. Again, he galloped over lotus leaves, without even pushing one of them under water."
- "The horse got upon it, and stood on the palm of his master's hand, with his four feet close together."

Story: A poor old woman keeps a little foal like her own son. She feeds him rice water, scraps and grass. Nobody knows
what he is. (Fail.) A horse trader stays at her house. His 500 horses will not go in while the foal is out. The trader
waits, sees the foal, reads his marks, and knows (Hero). He takes the foal with love and pays the old woman well.
Then he tests him. The foal runs so fast you see only a red leaf tied to his side. He runs over a pond and his hooves stay dry.
He runs over lotus leaves and not one goes under. Then he stands on his master's open hand, four feet together. (Payoff.)

Surprise: the fastest, strongest horse is also the lightest: dry hooves, lotus leaves that do not sink, a horse on a palm.

Lesson for practice: in Vatayanasana put the knee down like a foot on a lotus leaf, with no thump. Lightness is the
strength showing. Slot: [BOONCHU'S OWN CUE: 5 words or less].

Habit Gap: weak-medium. Someone saw his marks and tested him, "whether he knew his own power or no": a teacher shows you
a power you did not know you had. No quitting or coming back in it.
Lane A (STUCK) fits best (soft-knee fix). Hook bank #113, adapted ("The strongest horse in the story could stand on a hand.").

Safety: no deaths, no war. Leave out the king giving "the half of his kingdom" and the foal's proud verse
("I am chief of steeds ... I will not take this fare"), which reads as arrogance. The frame (Sariputta happily eats a poor
woman's food) is kind and can stay out. The trader is the Bodhisatta; respectful.

Risks: it overlaps C (both are horses, both "someone reads the marks"); pick one for Vatayanasana. It is weaker on the
Habit Gap than A, B or C.

## Rejected or risky, with the reason (do not pitch without Boonchu's say)

- Gajendra and the crocodile (Bhagavata Purana 8.2.27-8.3.33, vedabase.io): the fight lasts "one thousand years"; the elephant
  grows weaker, while the crocodile, "being an animal of the water, increased in enthusiasm, physical strength" (8.2.30).
  A great Habit Gap line (the one at home in its own water gets stronger). But in 8.3.33 "the Lord severed the crocodile's
  mouth from its body with His disc": a killing scene. Rejected.
- Arjuna and the five crocodiles (Mahabharata, Adi Parva, Ganguli secs. CCXVIII-CCXIX): the crocodiles "take away the ascetics"
  who bathe (victims); nymphs who tried to tempt a young Brahmana are cursed. Rejected (victims, temptation).
- Nacca Jataka No. 32 (Cowell I, pp. 83-84): the peacock wins the bride, then dances and "exposed himself"; the frame has a monk
  standing "stark naked". Rejected (not for a 5-year-old).
- Baveru Jataka No. 339 (Cowell III): the peacock makes people forget the crow; the frame mocks rival teachers ("the Jain
  Nathaputta was the crow"). Rejected (mocks a religion).
- Tittha Jataka No. 25 (Cowell I, pp. 64-66): the state horse will not bathe where a "sorry beast" was washed; "Change thou the spot".
  The frame: Sariputta gives a pupil the wrong meditation for four months and the Buddha fixes it with a lotus. Backup only:
  it clashes with "same practice, same time", may feel like it lowers Sariputta for Thai viewers, and has low surprise.
- Valmiki's curlews (Krounchasana) and the idle camel (Ustrasana): see the table in part 2. Both are killing scenes.

## Ranking

1. A, The Peacock Who Forgot Once (Pincha Mayurasana): strongest Habit Gap (skip once, come back), safe in the Chinese telling,
   ties to Thailand and China.
2. B, The Rock That Answered (Nakrasana): strongest "teacher who notices" story, funny, kid-safe, surprising.
3. C, The Thin Horses (Vatayanasana): good surprise, good breath line, name match "fleet as wind".
4. D, The Foal on Lotus Leaves (Vatayanasana backup): pretty and safe, weak Habit Gap.
