# Hidden Stories of Ashtanga · Ep. ? · "The Guest Who Ate Everything, Six Times" · Asana: Durvasasana (Advanced A, 3rd series, Sthira Bhaga A) · Lane B · ask word: aybkk

Writer's script, 9 Nov 2026, fixed the same day after the checker (see Fix log at the end). Story id C1 (key advA1). No episode number until it is filmed. Beat order H, DH, T1, RH, T2, P.

Lane B, and why: the point is the habit. You lose a week, then you go back with no anger. That is the Habit Gap. Durvasasana comes in as a picture of falling out and standing up again. It is not a fix for an asana people are stuck in. Lane A needs a real technique fix and the STUCK ask. Durvasasana is a 3rd series asana that few viewers practise, and this story gives no technique. So the caption asks for "aybkk" (keyword test, frozen until 22 Nov).

## 2. Stakes and why care

Stakes: Say you wait to feel like it after a lost week. Or you get angry at yourself. Then one sick week or one trip turns into a month off. Next year you start from day one again.

Why care: You have lost practice to a trip, a sick week or a busy month before. Coming back felt heavy, so you waited to feel like it. This story shows a poor man who lost half a month of food six times. Each time he went straight back to the field, hungry, with no anger.

## 3. Toolkit

Toolkit pick: /hook (contrarian lever, bank #107 [bold]) + Fork-First Story.
Why: #107 says advice the viewer likes and trusts out loud ("practise when you feel like it"), then flips it in the next line. That flip is the contrarian lever. The story has a real fork built in. Half a month of food is gone, so what do you do? The viewer chooses before Mudgala does (Harvard #1). Then the twist lands as the rehook at about 0:26 to 0:30. The guest comes back, Mudgala gives again, and the guest is Durvasa, the sage famous for his anger. The point is out by about 0:07.5.

Toolkit choice:
- Vibe Samurai (flat statement "Feeling like it is a bad alarm clock" + a ladder of three lost pots + the turn): the ladder is strong, but the turn pulls toward a banned "not X but Y" line. Also, "Motivation is a bad alarm clock" is already Carnegie's Letter 01 (2 Nov).
- Gervais deadpan opener ("Guest eats your dinner. You say welcome. Six times."): dry and funny. But a joke this early tips into laughing at Durvasa, which is banned, and it gives away the twist.

## 4. Hook bank

Bank line, #107 [bold] fit=adapt goal=shala habit=3:
- template: "{Popular advice} is ruining {group}"
- aybkk: "'Practise when you feel like it' is ruining your yoga habit."
- note: "Aim at the advice, never at a studio, teacher or app that gives it."

How I adapted it:
- Used almost as written, split in two. Line 1 says the advice by itself, so the viewer nods ("yes, listen to your body"). Line 2 flips it ("That's ruining your yoga habit."). That is the contrarian move. "[bold]" is the hook bank's tag for #107. The /hook lever used is contrarian.
- "Is ruining" became "That's ruining" so it fits his short spoken voice. The words "yoga habit" stay.
- It aims only at the advice. No studio, teacher or app is named or shown.
- Line 3 is the double hook. It is a story fact that raises the stakes and opens the loop: why on purpose, and what did the poor man do? Lines 12 to 16 pay it.
- The proof for the hook comes at line 18. Mudgala was hungry and insulted, so nobody would feel like it, and he went back anyway (Harvard #27).

Backup for the 5-hook test, #83 [story] fit=use goal=shala habit=3 ("How do you start over after years of starting yoga and quitting?"), adapted: "How you start over? One man did it six times." It is softer and less contrarian, so it is the backup only. Not used: #121 and #101 (Ep. 6 has them).

## 5. Harvard tricks used

- #1 Decide before you know the ending: line 11, "Half a month, gone. What you do?", then a 0.5 s pause with eyes in the lens. Line 12 answers it ("Mudgala give again."), and line 14 finishes the answer.
- #27 Every claim needs a because: line 18 is the because for the hook's claim (lines 1 and 2). "Hungry. Who feel like it? Mudgala go back anyway." Line 16 is the because for "on purpose" (line 3). He came to look for anger, and the book says he saw no change in Mudgala's mind (3.246.21 to 22). Line 19's name claim has its because in the caption source (Iyengar 1979, Monier-Williams).
- #21 Teach them something about their own problem: lines 18 and 20. The feeling never shows up on the hard days, and the anger after a lost week is what keeps you away. The asana carries the angry sage's name, but standing up again needs the man with no anger.
- #30 Sell only the next step: the spoken video sells nothing. No price, no course, no link line. The caption has ONE ask (comment aybkk).

Not used: #2 (a result-for-your-life line would be a pitch, and Lane B keeps the pitch out of the video). #20 (the close asks what you will do next time, not what it costs. A cost line would repeat the Stakes and push the take past 60 s).

## 8. Caption (English)

<p>Hidden Stories of Ashtanga · Ep. ?</p>
<p>The Guest Who Ate Everything, Six Times</p>
<p>Practise when you feel like it? That advice is ruining your yoga habit.</p>
<p>Mudgala was a poor, wise man.</p>
<p>After the harvest, he picked up the grains the farmers left on the field. Like a pigeon.</p>
<p>Half a month of picking gave him one small measure of rice.</p>
<p>On moon days, he fed his guests first. He lived on what was left.</p>
<p>One day a guest came. He looked wild, and he shouted rude words.</p>
<p>He said he came for food. Mudgala made him welcome.</p>
<p>The guest ate all of it. Then he left, just as he came.</p>
<p>Next moon day, he came back. Mudgala gave again. The guest ate it all again.</p>
<p>He came six times. His mind was made up.</p>
<p>The guest was Durvasa, a sage famous for his anger. A sage is a wise holy man.</p>
<p>Each time, Mudgala ate nothing himself.</p>
<p>He went back to the field with his wife and son, and picked grains again.</p>
<p>Hunger could not change him. Anger could not get in.</p>
<p>Durvasa looked for any change in Mudgala's mind. He found none.</p>
<p>He was pleased. He said nobody on earth gives like Mudgala.</p>
<p>Then a messenger of the gods came in a sky chariot.</p>
<p>Swans and cranes pulled it. It came to take Mudgala to heaven.</p>
<p>The messenger told him about heaven. There you only use up what you earned.</p>
<p>When it runs out, you fall.</p>
<p>So Mudgala said, "I do not desire heaven."</p>
<p>He let the messenger go. He stayed calm and sat in meditation every day.</p>
<p>Durvasasana (also written Durvasana) is the asana named after Durvasa.</p>
<p>You stand on one leg, with the other foot behind your head.</p>
<p>You fall out. You stand up again. No anger.</p>
<p>Feeling lazy is one thing. Pain is another.</p>
<p>Aching, yes. Sharp pain, no. That one, you tell your teacher.</p>
<p>This story is in the Mahabharata, India's great old story. It is in Book 3, the Forest Book.</p>
<p>In K. M. Ganguli's English, it is sections 258 and 259.</p>

## 10. Flags

1. "Pot" (lines 3, 7, 12, 15) is a picture word. The text says one drona, which Ganguli's fn 85 calls "a very small measure". The caption says "one small measure". If a purist objects, say "a little rice".
2. Ganguli and the critical text differ on the eating. Ganguli says the family "ate for a fortnight, and during the other fortnight" gleaned. The critical text (3.246.5) says he gathered a drona in half a month and ate once in half a month. The script says only "half a month, one small pot", which is true for both. I left out "eat once in half a month", so it cannot read as fasting advice.
3. "Next moon day" (line 12) follows the Sanskrit "dvitiye samprapte parvakale" (when the second moon day came). Ganguli says "during the next season". Never say "same day" or "next morning". Moon days here are the new and full moon rites (darsha and paurnamasa, 3.246.6), so six visits are about three months, not six. That is why no moon is drawn on screen (fix, 9 Nov): six full moons would read as six months. The six empty pots carry the count instead, one per visit. The moon gloss is mine and is not on screen.
4. The story ends differently in the two texts. The critical text says he gave up gleaning at the end (3.247.41). Ganguli still calls him "that virtuous one leading the Unchha mode of life". The spoken story ends at Durvasa's praise, and the caption ends with meditation. The close now says "Back to the mat?" (fix, 9 Nov), so it can't be heard as the story's ending.
5. Durvasa's look. "Digvasas" means naked (fn 87), so never say it and never show it. Ganguli reads "vikaca" as "his head bare of hair", while the pick sheet said "hair wild". So draw no hair at all. He is a shadow at the door or a far figure from behind. "Looking wild" stays in the caption only, as the brief asks.
6. Lines 3, 12 and 16 are retold. The text says he came six times "his mind made up" and saw no change (vikara) in Mudgala's mind. "On purpose" (line 3) and "look for anger" (line 16) are my plain words for that, and anger (krodha) is named in 3.246.20. "Mudgala give again" (line 12) is retold from Ganguli's "ate up all the food supplied by that wise one" and from 3.246.8 (he gave food to guests on the moon days). The caption says "looked for any change", which is closer. No quote marks on these lines. The word "test" is never used.
7. "Famous angry sage" follows MW ("known for his irascibility") and Iyengar ("a proverbially angry sage"). Durvasa is a revered rishi, so no cartoon anger, no joke, and never "crazy" or "mad". Ganguli's "maniac" and "frantic" stay out. The name tag explains "sage" the first time it is heard (WISE HOLY MAN).
8. The leftovers line is gone from the caption (fix, 9 Nov). It is true (3.246.17), but it is not needed, and Thai and Indian readers can read it as disrespect to a sage. It is now in the left-out list.
9. The asana lines are a teaching picture, not from a source. "You fall out. Stand up again." is Plato's words, so Boonchu should confirm it from his room or replace it with his own cue. AYI gives only the rise cue and the hands-on-thigh help.

Nothing here says to practise when sick or in pain. "Bad week" is said in general and never names an illness.
