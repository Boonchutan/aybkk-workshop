First, the limits Boone Jai Setting it up

A nightly routine · Two children

One tutor who listens. One who tells stories.

Boone reads out loud and gets interrupted on the word he stumbles over. Jai is three and cannot read at all, so hers does the opposite — it tells her a story and lets her finish it. Both keep a log only you ever see.

You read the log. They never do. The log is a list of the words that went wrong. That is a useful thing for a parent and a discouraging thing for a child.
Nobody tells them it is a reading programme. As far as Boone is concerned he tells a computer about dinosaurs. The moment it feels like school, it is over.

Read this before you trust the log

It hears him. It cannot see the page.
It has no idea what is actually printed in the book. If Boone skips a line, or swaps in a word that still makes sense, it will never know. The log records what it heard, not what he read.
Speech recognition mishears small bilingual children.
This is the main way the whole thing goes wrong — the tutor "corrects" a word he read perfectly well, and he decides he is bad at reading. Both prompts below carry an explicit instruction to ask him to say it again rather than assume a mistake. Don't delete that line.
Jai is three, so an adult sits with her.
Ten minutes, and stop early if she drifts. She is not being taught to read. She is being read to and talked with, which is the thing that actually predicts reading later.
Boone · Foundation, reads above his year

He reads out loud. It stops him on the word, not the page.

One word, sounded out properly, then the whole sentence again from the start so the meaning closes up behind it. It never quizzes him at the end — it asks what he thinks happens next.

Nightly · about fifteen minutes

Change the second sentence if his reading has moved on — it is the line that decides everything else.

A short story about a rabbit, stopped halfway so she decides what happens next. One small sound game. Then she tells the whole thing back. That is the entire session, and at three it is the part that matters.

Setting it up

Once per child, then it runs itself. Do it twice — the two tutors must not share a chat, or Jai's log ends up in Boone's book choices.

  1. Make a Project for each childIn the Claude app: Projects, then New project. Call one Boone and one Jai.
  2. Paste the nightly prompt into the project's instructionsNot into a message — into the project instructions, so every new chat already knows who it is talking to.
  3. Start a chat inside that project and turn on voiceHand it over and let them talk to it. The screen can lie face down; only the microphone matters.
  4. Once a week, open a new chat in the same projectPaste the week's PARENT LOG entries, then the weekly pick prompt underneath them.

Both prompts are also saved in the workshop repo at kids/reading-tutor.md, so they can be edited there and stay in version control alongside the two playground apps.
